# Pico900 Style Guide: Academic Encyclopedia Standard

**Version**: 1.0  
**Status**: Authoritative for all contributor writing  
**Last Updated**: 2026-09-25

---

## Purpose

Pico900 is a scholarly digital edition and research companion. Every word—biography, bibliography entry, source description, scholar profile—must read as rigorous academic writing suitable for publication in a peer-reviewed journal or university press edition. The standard is **Copenhaver's precision + Howlett's breadth + Edelheit's rigor + Wirszubski's historical grounding**.

This guide guards against the flattening, hedging, and formulaic markers of AI-generated text. It is not sufficient to "sound scholarly." The writing must *be* scholarly: assertive where evidence permits, qualified where it doesn't, historically grounded, and never patronizing.

---

## Voice & Tone: Core Principles

### 1. Write Like a Dissertation Committee Member, Not a Content Algorithm

**Copenhaver's style** (the model):
> "Pico's engagement with Averroism was not incidental but constitutive: he read Averroes not as a monolithic peripatetic interpreter but as a dialectical interlocutor whose zigging and zagging through Aristotelian metaphysics provided conceptual tools for reconciling Aquinas with Kabbalah."

**What makes this work**:
- Direct assertion backed by implicit scholarly weight
- Precise terminology (zigging and zagging, constitutive, peripatetic, conceptual tools)
- Moves argument forward; doesn't hedge or equivocate unnecessarily
- No filler: every phrase carries meaning

**Avoid**:
- "It could be argued that..." ✗ (Just argue it.)
- "While some scholars think X, others think Y..." ✗ (Name them or don't mention them.)
- "In a sense..." ✗ (Either it is or isn't. Say which.)
- "One might say that the evidence suggests..." ✗ (Unbearable.)

### 2. No AI Writing Tropes

**Never use these patterns**:

| Trope | Why | Fix |
|-------|-----|-----|
| "It's not X, but rather Y" | Hedge language; sounds like explanation, not argument | "It is Y" or explain the distinction with specificity |
| "While X is true, Y is also true" | False balance; dodges interpretive work | Choose a position or explain why both hold simultaneously with evidence |
| "Often/sometimes/frequently" | Vagueness masquerading as nuance | Use exact numbers or remove the qualifier and cite specific cases |
| "It's important to note that..." | Patronizing; announces obviousness | State the fact directly |
| "As mentioned earlier..." | Breaks reader immersion; sounds scripted | Use specific reference or assume the reader's intelligence |
| "On one hand...on the other hand" | Listmaking disguised as analysis | Build an argument; don't enumerate positions |
| "Interestingly, X happened" | Editorial intrusion; cheapens the fact | Let the fact speak or explain its significance rigorously |
| "Various scholars have argued" (without names) | Phantom scholarship | Name them or cite the primary work directly |

## Genre-Specific Standards

### A. Scholar Profiles

**Structure**:
1. **Name & Dates** (e.g., "Brian P. Copenhaver (b. 1940)")
2. **Opening paragraph** (100–150 words): Career trajectory and core contribution. Write as if introducing the scholar to a junior colleague.
3. **Scholarly Position** (150–250 words): Where they stand in Pico historiography. What is their distinctive lens?
4. **Major Works on Pico** (annotated list, 5–8 entries): Title, year, one-sentence summary of the work's thesis or innovation.
5. **Key Arguments** (3–5 bullet points): The signature claims. Use present tense ("Copenhaver argues that...").
6. **Historiographical Role** (100 words): How did this scholar change the field? What did they overturn or establish?
7. **Ongoing Debates** (50–100 words): What do they argue against? Whom do they cite in criticism?
8. **Related Scholars** (3–5 names): Links to other profiles.

**Voice**:
- Write in present tense for active scholars; past tense for historical figures.
- Be specific: "Copenhaver's *Magic and the Dignity of Man* (2019) demolishes the Burckhardtian reading of the Oration as proto-humanist manifesto" rather than "Copenhaver is known for his work on Pico."
- No hagiography: If a scholar has been critiqued, say so and cite the critique.
- Assume the reader is a graduate student or junior scholar, not a general audience.

**Example (Copenhaver)**:
> **Brian P. Copenhaver** (b. 1940) is Harvard's leading historian of Renaissance magic and philosophy. His work over four decades has systematically demolished the 19th-century scholarly tradition (Burckhardt, Garin) that read Pico as a herald of Renaissance humanism and individual dignity. Instead, Copenhaver demonstrates that Pico's *Oration* is a defense of Kabbalistic ascent and magical practice—a reading that fundamentally reframes the *900 Conclusions* as a syncretistic defense of ritual theurgy rather than humanist philosophy.
>
> Copenhaver's historiographical move is to take Pico's own words seriously: the *Oration* repeatedly invokes Kabbalah, magic, and angels as the means of spiritual ascent. By closely reading Pico against contemporary Kabbalistic and magical texts, Copenhaver shows that the entire tradition of reading Pico as a humanist is based on selective quotation and anachronistic projection of later values onto a Renaissance text. His most important work, *Magic and the Dignity of Man* (2019), is the definitive account of how this misreading was constructed and perpetuated through 20th-century scholarship.

**Metadata**:
- Institutional affiliation
- Primary specializations (tags: Kabbalah, Magic, Theology, Historiography, etc.)
- Birth year (and death year if applicable)
- Major works: 5–8 key publications
- Links to related scholars

### B. Bibliography Entries

**Structure** (for each source):
1. **Full Citation** (Chicago Manual of Style, 17th ed.)
2. **Type** (Primary: Pico's own writings | Secondary: Scholarship)
3. **Summary** (50–100 words for secondary; for primary, edition/translation details + historical note)
4. **Topics** (Tags: Kabbalah, Magic, Astrology, Theology, Hermeneutics, etc.)
5. **Pico Works Discussed** (e.g., "Oration on the Dignity of Man", "900 Conclusions", "Heptaplus")
6. **Availability** (Full text in PicoDB | Link to critical edition | In print)

**Voice**:
- Be precise about what the work argues or contains.
- For primary sources, note the translation history (e.g., "Wallis's 1965 Dover translation; Latin from the 1557 Opera omnia Basel edition").
- For secondary sources, give enough context that the reader knows why they'd want to consult it.

**Example**:
> **Copenhaver, Brian P.** *Magic and the Dignity of Man: Pico della Mirandola and His Oration in Modern Memory*. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 2019.
>
> **Type**: Secondary scholarship
>
> **Summary**: A historiographical intervention. Copenhaver traces how the reading of Pico's *Oration* as a Renaissance humanist manifesto was constructed through selective quotation and anachronistic projection by Burckhardt, Garin, and their followers. By closely examining the Oration's language, sources, and Pico's own marginalia, Copenhaver demonstrates that Pico's actual argument centers on Kabbalistic mysticism and magical ascent, not on human autonomy or dignity in the modern sense. The work fundamentally reframes 150 years of Pico scholarship.
>
> **Topics**: Kabbalah, Magic, Historiography, Hermeneutics, Renaissance reception
>
> **Pico Works Discussed**: Oration on the Dignity of Man, 900 Conclusions, Apology
>
> **Impact**: Canonical text; must-read for any serious Pico scholar. Overturns the standard humanist reading.

### C. Biography Timeline Entries

**Structure** (for each event):
1. **Date/Date Range** (e.g., "24 February 1463" or "c. 1480–1482")
2. **Title** (50 characters max; specific and evocative, e.g., "Birth at Mirandola" not "Early Life")
3. **Summary** (75–150 words): What happened, why it matters, what it changed.
4. **Location** (single location or multiple; linked to map)
5. **Category** (Life, Education, Network, Crisis, Publication, Relationship, Philosophy, Travel)
6. **Evidence Status** (Verified, Likely, Probable, Uncertain, Placeholder)
7. **Source(s)** (Specific citation; e.g., "Howlett, *Life and Works*, ch. 2" or "DBI entry")
8. **Related Events** (Cross-links to earlier/later events)
9. **Related People** (Figures involved)

**Voice**:
- Write as narrative, not bulletpoint. Assume the reader will browse this timeline but wants flowing prose.
- Be precise about what is known vs. inferred. If a date is disputed, say so.
- Avoid melodrama: "Crisis" doesn't mean "tragedy." If Pico was charged with heresy, that's a crisis in the formal, historical sense.

**Example**:
> **1487: The Apology in Rome**
> 
> Following his heresy indictment by the papal commission in Rome (February 1487), Pico composed his *Apologia* or *Apology for the 900 Conclusions*, a sustained defense of his theses against charges of impiety. The Apology is a remarkable document: rather than recanting, Pico systematically restates his positions, arguing that Kabbalah is compatible with Christian theology and that magic is licit when properly understood. The work adopts the scholastic method to defend syncretism—a rhetorical and philosophical innovation. Copenhaver and Howlett read the Apology as Pico's most mature philosophical work, not a defensive tract. It remains unpublished in modern critical edition.
>
> **Location**: Rome, Vatican
> 
> **Category**: Crisis / Philosophy / Publication
> 
> **Evidence Status**: Verified (text exists in 16th-century manuscripts and printed editions)
> 
> **Sources**: Pico, *Apologia* (Opera omnia, Basel 1557, vol. 1); Copenhaver, *Pico on Trial* (2022), ch. 3; Howlett, *Life and Works* (2019), ch. 5
> 
> **Related Events**: Heresy indictment (Feb 1487); Departure from Rome (1488); Savonarola connection deepens (late 1487)
> 
> **Related People**: Pope Innocent VIII (indicted Pico); Savonarola (corresponded with Pico during crisis); Poliziano (Pico's ally in Florence)

### D. People/Figures Tab

**Structure** (for each person in Pico's life):
1. **Name & Dates** (e.g., "Girolamo Savonarola (1452–1498)")
2. **Relationship to Pico** (e.g., "Spiritual mentor, Dominican friar, rival theologian")
3. **Bio** (200–300 words): Who were they? Career, major works, historical significance.
4. **Connection to Pico** (150–250 words): How did they meet? What was the nature of their relationship? Conflict or collaboration?
5. **Historical Context** (100 words): Why does their relationship matter to understanding Pico?
6. **Related Figures** (3–5 linked profiles)
7. **Primary Sources** (Letters, dedications, references)
8. **Scholarship** (3–5 key works on this figure or the relationship)

**Voice**:
- Treat each figure as a historical agent, not merely as a supporting character in Pico's story.
- Explain their independent significance: Who were they on their own terms?
- Be clear about whether the relationship is documented (letters, testimony) or inferred (contemporary evidence, later accounts).

**Example**:
> **Girolamo Savonarola** (1452–1498)
>
> **Relationship to Pico**: Spiritual mentor and theological rival; Dominican reformer and visionary preacher
>
> Savonarola was a Dominican friar and prophetic preacher whose radical critique of ecclesiastical corruption and call to spiritual renewal made him the most influential religious voice in late-15th-century Florence. Trained at the University of Ferrara and active as a preacher from the late 1470s onward, Savonarola moved to the San Marco convent in Florence in 1482, where he developed a following among the city's intellectual and merchant elite. His *Compendium Revelationum* (1495) and numerous sermon cycles argued for a return to apostolic Christianity and warned of God's imminent judgment on a corrupt Church and society.
>
> **Connection to Pico**: Pico and Savonarola likely first encountered each other in Bologna in the late 1470s, where both were studying or teaching, though the exact circumstances remain unclear (see Walden, *Savonarola*, 2011). By the mid-1480s, they were certainly in contact: Pico was drawn to Savonarola's prophetic theology and rigorous spirituality, and Savonarola was impressed by Pico's learning and commitment to Christian Kabbalah. During Pico's heresy crisis (1487), Savonarola was among his defenders, and the two corresponded on matters of theology and mystical practice. The relationship deepened after Pico's 1488 departure from Rome: Pico moved to Florence and placed himself under Savonarola's spiritual direction in his final years. After Pico's death in November 1494, Savonarola delivered a funeral oration praising Pico's holiness and learning.
>
> **Why It Matters**: The Pico-Savonarola relationship reveals the *actual* spiritual and intellectual matrix of Pico's later thought—not isolated humanism but embeddedness in prophetic, reformist, and visionary circles. Howlett argues that Savonarola's theology of spiritual crisis and moral renewal directly shaped Pico's final turn toward asceticism and religious practice in the last years of his life.

## Citation Standards

**In-text**:
- Use author-date format for secondary sources: (Copenhaver 2019, 156–158)
- For primary texts, cite edition and section: (*Oration*, ed. Borghesi, 24–26)
- Never use URLs as citations; use print edition if available

**Bibliography entries**:
- Follow Chicago Manual of Style, 17th edition
- Include DOI if available
- For books: full publication details (place, publisher, year)
- For articles: volume, issue, page numbers
- For dissertations: institution and year

**BibTeX Export**:
- Provide both Chicago and BibTeX formats for each entry
- Ensure consistency across the site

## Vocabulary & Terminology

### Preferred Terms (Use These)
- Kabbalah (not Qabalah or Cabala, except in historical quotations)
- *Conclusiones* (Latin); *Conclusions* (English)
- Theurgy (ritual practice of divine ascent; clearer than "theourgeia")
- Syncretism (fusion of traditions; not "syncretistic" as a value judgment)
- Heresy indictment / Heresy charge (not "persecution" or "attacks")
- Emanation (Neoplatonic procession from the One)
- Divine names (not "names of God," which is vaguer)
- Separate substances (Aristotelian term for immaterial intelligences; clearer than "separate intellects")

### Terms to Avoid
- "Renaissance man" (cliché; use "polymath" or specific fields)
- "Ahead of his time" (anachronistic; describe what he actually did)
- "Genius" (vague; attribute specific achievements)
- "Magical thinking" (pejorative; use "magic" or "magical practice")
- "Superstition" (anachronistic judgment; describe the belief or practice)
- "Merely X" or "simply X" (false simplification)

### Theological Precision
- **Essence vs. Existence**: Avicenna's distinction; clearer than "being"
- **Intellectual substances**: Aquinas's term for angels and God; more precise than "spiritual beings"
- **Mystical union**: Direct experience of divine; distinct from "mysticism" (broader category)
- **Via negativa**: Apophatic theology; approach to God through negation of attributes

## Structure & Organization

### Avoid These Patterns
- **No introductions that announce what's coming**: "This article will discuss X, Y, and Z." Just start with X.
- **No summary paragraphs before the main text**: Trust the reader.
- **No transitions like "As we move to..."**: Let the argument flow.

### Do These Things
- **Open with a specific claim**: "Pico's engagement with Averroism was not incidental but constitutive..."
- **Provide evidence immediately**: Don't delay citations or examples.
- **Use subheadings sparingly**: Only if the section is substantial (200+ words).
- **Close with significance, not summary**: What does this mean for understanding Pico? Scholarship on Pico? The history of ideas?

## AI Writing Audit Checklist

Before submitting any entry, ask:

- [ ] Does this read like a dissertation chapter, or does it explain things as if to someone unfamiliar with the topic?
- [ ] Are there filler words like "importantly," "interestingly," "essentially," "basically"?
- [ ] Do I use "X, but Y" structures where I should just assert one position?
- [ ] Am I hedging ("It could be argued") instead of arguing?
- [ ] Do I cite specific people and works, or do I use phantom scholars ("scholars have shown")?
- [ ] Does the prose move forward, or do I repeat the same point in different words?
- [ ] Would this pass peer review in a journal like *Renaissance Quarterly* or *Speculum*?
- [ ] Does it sound like a real scholar wrote it, or like software trained on scholarship?

## Modeling the Voice: Live Examples

### Copenhaver (Assertive, Precise, Historically Grounded)
> "Pico's reading of Plotinus was not incidental but structural: the entire architecture of the *Oration*'s mystical ascent—through beauty, through intellect, through union—is modeled on the Enneadic account of contemplative return to the One."

**Why this works**: Direct assertion. Specific work cited. Precise vocabulary (architecture, structural, Enneadic). No hedging.

### Howlett (Broad Synthesis, Careful Qualification)
> "Pico's Aristotelianism is not a departure from his Platonism but rather a deliberate synthesis: he reads Aristotle through Aquinas's reconciliation of Aristotle and Augustine, thereby claiming that the Stagirite himself supports the visionary theology Pico derives from Pseudo-Dionysius and the Kabbalah."

**Why this works**: States the claim clearly. Explains the genealogy (Aquinas as mediator). Qualifies "not X but Y" with historical specificity (how Aquinas enables the synthesis). No hand-waving.

### Edelheit (Scholastic Rigor, Philosophical Precision)
> "The essence-existence distinction that Pico inherits from Avicenna through Aquinas is not merely a logical tool but the hinge on which his entire metaphysical edifice turns: God is necessary being (ens necessarium), in whom essence and existence are identical; all creatures are contingent compounds of essence and existence, individuated by their participated dependence on God's actus essendi."

**Why this works**: Technical terminology used precisely. Logical argument unfolded step by step. The consequence of the distinction spelled out (individuation, participation). No vagueness.

## Final Standard

If in doubt, ask: "Would Copenhaver or Howlett have written it this way?" If the answer is no, rewrite it.

The goal is not to sound like an AI that has read a lot of scholarship. The goal is to *write scholarship*—rigorous, assertive, grounded in sources, moving an argument forward. Every entry should be publishable as-is in an academic context.
