# Being, the One, and the Path to God: Pico's *De ente et uno*

This essay has five stops: the concordance project and the Parmenides question; the four transcendentals in creatures; the four steps to God and apophatic silence; God in himself versus God as cause; and the connections to the *Heptaplus* and Pseudo-Dionysius, with the tensions the text leaves open (ed.).

## Stop 1 — The question and the Parmenides

*De ente et uno* opens with the concordance project as its stated purpose [[wallis1965-deente:002]]. Pico describes his own philosophy as one that makes a concordant philosophy of Plato and Aristotle, and says those who hold that Aristotle disagrees with Plato disagree with him [[wallis1965-deente:002]]. The letter that begins the treatise was provoked by Poliziano, who had put the question of how Aristotle might agree with his master Plato — a question on which, as Pico frames it, a misreading of two Platonic dialogues had thrown the entire tradition off course [[wallis1965-deente:002]].

The two places where Plato disputed on being and the one are the Parmenides and the Sophist, and the Academics take Plato in both to have placed the one above being [[wallis1965-deente:011]]. Edelheit summarises Pico's diagnosis: where the Academics regard the Parmenides as a dogmatic dialogue containing Plato's doctrine of the one and being, Pico presents a more literal interpretation, taking seriously what the dialogue actually says and attending to the implications of its conclusions; according to Pico, the entire dialogue is nothing but an exercise in dialectic or logic [[edelheit2022:006]]. To show why, Pico quotes Zeno's remark that if Parmenides's subject were divine orders and the first principle of all things it would be a most suitable subject for an old man and one he need not be ashamed of — the point being that Plato himself signals through Zeno that the dialogue is a training in argument, not an exposition of first philosophy [[wallis1965-deente:015]].

The key distinction Pico draws is between two things that look similar but are not (ed.). To assert that the one is above being is not the same as to assert, hypothetically, that if all things were one, that one would not be being [[wallis1965-deente:018]]. The second assertion is conditional; even if the Parmenides contains doctrine, its hypothetical conclusions cannot be read as categorical statements about the one's transcendence [[wallis1965-deente:018]]. Pico grants, for argument's sake, that Plato affirmed the one above being — conceding something he says Plato nowhere affirmed — and asks in what sense Plato could have spoken truly, first laying down the Aristotelian foundations [[wallis1965-deente:021]]. The Platonists, as Pico reads them, hold the one superior to being not in the sense in which he himself agrees with it, but in an absolute sense that goes against Aristotle [[wallis1965-deente:038]].

## Stop 2 — The four transcendentals in creatures

The argument that allows Pico to accept a qualified superiority of the one over being begins with the four transcendentals in creatures (ed.). Being comes first: each thing is conceived under being because the agent produces the effect before the effect is anything in itself, and nothing after God is understood except as finite, participated being; one follows being, and truth is third, because after something is in itself it must be seen whether it matches the exemplar according to which it was formed [[wallis1965-deente:078]]. Being and the one are the same in extension: whoever gives something being also makes it one, and whoever does not say one says nothing, as Plato says in the Sophist [[wallis1965-deente:079]].

The four transcendentals can be traced back from lower to higher by a process of abstraction (ed.). Life is a being and therefore a one, a perfection; wisdom is likewise one perfection; take away the particularity, and there remains not this or that one but the one itself and the one absolutely; in the same way good absolutely and truth itself emerge, so that God is being itself, the one itself, the good itself, and truth itself [[wallis1965-deente:049]]. One, true, and good follow being by perpetual connection, so that when we are not these three we absolutely are not, even if we appear to be; and although we may seem to live, we would be ever dying rather than living [[wallis1965-deente:099]].

Howlett notes that the *De ente et uno* yields a God who is being itself, one itself, truth itself and good itself in this strong sense, while also placing God above each of them — a tension she identifies as central to Pico's metaphysics [[howlett2021:106]]. Miller observes that the two Greek philosophies Pico is reconciling can be made to coincide only when transformed: Plato's view that being is a relation derived from the pure relation of identity, or the one, is incompatible with Aristotle's view that being means the concrete individual substance, and the reconciliation requires identifying the highest principle of each with a God who is existence itself [[wallis1965-deente:115|wallis1965-deente:116]].

## Stop 3 — The four steps to God and beyond

The argument Pico makes turns on a particular understanding of what a name like "being" means when predicated of God (ed.). These names — being, true, one, good — mean something concrete, participated, so Pico says God is above being, above true, above one, above good, since he is existence itself, truth itself, unity itself, goodness itself; but we are still in light, and God has established his dwelling in darkness [[wallis1965-deente:051]].

The reason God is above being in this sense is that God is existence itself — *ipsum esse* — of himself and from himself, the source of all things without an interposing medium, the plenitude of all existence; by participation in which all things are; so we truly say he is not being but above being, and that something is higher than being [[wallis1965-deente:031]]. The fourth step carries this further (ed.). Pico says we must rise into the light of ignorance, blinded by the darkness of divine splendour, and finally say only that God is unintelligibly and ineffably above everything most perfect that we can either speak or conceive; at this step we place God most eminently above even unity, goodness, truth and existence as we conceive them [[wallis1965-deente:052]].

Howlett identifies the second argument — that God is not being but is above being and is the one above being — with the Ein-Sof and also with the Plotinian One, a God above all things; she notes that this apparently contradicts Pico's first argument, and leaves the question of whether the two arguments represent a genuine contradiction or two aspects of the same God open [[howlett2021:132]]. Miller argues that Pico's concord is accomplished by transforming both philosophies into a different metaphysics in which the highest principle of each is identified with a God who is existence itself [[wallis1965-deente:116]].

Miller's note on chapter 5 adds that Pico, in his treatment of being in the second sense, draws on the metaphysics of Aquinas, who was the first Christian philosopher to base his metaphysics on the real distinction between that which participates existence and the act of existing itself [[wallis1965-deente:109]]. This grounding shows in the structure: Pico's thesis that essence and existence in any created being are really distinguished — Conclusiones thesis 31 — is traced by Edelheit to Thomas's *De ente et essentia*, where in God alone are essence and existence identical while in created intellectual substances existence is limited by the receiving nature [[edelheit2022:072]]. Edelheit traces the further claim, that everything around God that is actual is a composite of essence and existence, God alone being pure existence from which all created existence derives, to the correspondence with Cittadini [[edelheit2022:031]].

Even the angels do not escape the dependence (ed.). Pico says in the *De ente* that the life of the angels is not perfect: unless the vivifying ray of divine light constantly warmed it, it would all fall into nothingness [[wallis1965-deente:043]]. This statement appears in chapter 5 and is one of only two passages on angels in the body of the treatise; it connects the metaphysics of participated existence directly to the condition of separate substances, but does not elaborate on angelic orders or hierarchy [[wallis1965-deente:043]].

## Stop 4 — God in himself and as cause

The four transcendentals can be considered in God in two ways, and the treatment differs sharply depending on which way is chosen (ed.). They are not in God in relation to a cause, since God, the cause of all things, is from nothing; they can be considered either in God as he consists absolutely in himself, or in God as he is the cause of other things; this distinction does not apply to creatures, since God can choose not to be a cause while creatures cannot fail to be from him [[wallis1965-deente:087]].

When the four are assigned to God as he is the cause of things, the sequence is reversed; God would be first one, since he is understood in himself before he is understood as cause; second he would be good; third true; fourth being; the reason for the reversal is that the cause called the end is prior to the exemplary cause, and the exemplary cause prior to the efficient cause, so good pertains to the final cause, true to the exemplary, being to the efficient; Pico notes that these points remain full of many and great questions [[wallis1965-deente:091]].

Blum's essay points out that on the surface *De ente et uno* was a polemical correction of Ficino's Platonism, but that it also defends the same view of God and his engagement in creatures as the *Heptaplus*: God's attributes are one in God through a simple, highest, ineffable, originative unity, and are consequently above all things and outside all things, yet in such a way that they are very profound in all things and more one with all things than they are with themselves [[dougherty2008:030]]. Borghesi adds that even if the thesis of *De ente et uno* contained nothing new, Pico's reevaluation of the Parmenides, Sophist and Philebus and Simplicius's commentaries on Aristotle was new, and his project to read Plato through both Aristotle and medieval theologians as well as through Plotinus and Proclus remained unpublished [[dougherty2008:061]].

## Stop 5 — Dionysius, the *Heptaplus*, and what remains open

*De ente et uno* explicitly reconciles Plato, Aristotle, and Pseudo-Dionysius on the question whether God is intellect (ed.). Plato, Pico says, does not disagree with Aristotle when in the Republic he places God above intellect and intelligible things; Aristotle often calls God both intellect and intelligible; and Dionysius, although he says the same thing as Plato, does not deny with Aristotle that God is not ignorant of himself and other things [[wallis1965-deente:062]]. If intellect is taken as a nature tending outside itself to the intelligible as to another thing, Aristotle no less than the Platonists will deny that God is intellect and intelligible in that sense [[wallis1965-deente:063]]. The appearance of disagreement evaporates once the sense of the predication is made clear (ed.).

Howlett describes the intellectual environment in which this reconciliation made sense: Pico's universe joins the Latin emphasis on positive theology with the negative theology prominent in Byzantine Orthodoxy, at a time of renewed interest in Pseudo-Dionysius [[howlett2021:010]]. Edelheit reports Ficino's own version of the connection: Ficino claimed to have found that the chief mysteries of Numenius, Philo, Plotinus, Iamblichus and Proclus were taken from John, Paul, Hierotheus and Dionysius the Areopagite, and that whatever they said about the divine mind and the angels was taken from those figures [[edelheit2008:036]].

In the *Heptaplus*, written within a year or two of the *Conclusiones*, Pico extends the Dionysian frame into biblical exegesis (ed.). Pico says that much about the angelic and invisible nature had been handed down by the ancient Hebrews and by Dionysius, and that his plan was to expound the words of Moses according to the teaching of both schools [[wallis1965-heptaplus:058]]. He decided to put off the Hebrew teaching because it is unfamiliar to the Latins and would require explaining nearly all the dogmas of the ancient Hebrew learning, which he says he will publish more fully elsewhere, examining how far they agree with the traditions of Egypt, the philosophy of Plato, and Catholic truth [[wallis1965-heptaplus:059]]. For the Third Exposition, Pico says he will tread in the footsteps of Dionysius, or rather Paul and Hierotheus, whom Dionysius followed, to bring light on the shadows of the law [[wallis1965-heptaplus:061]]. Black identifies the structural connection between the two works: the core of the *Heptaplus*'s theory of allegory derives from the Proclean-Dionysian link between epistemology and ontology, so that emanation from God down a hierarchical chain is matched by a reversion along the same chain by way of knowledge, culminating in union with God [[black2006-c:098]].

The *Heptaplus*'s Sixth Exposition proem takes the unity of creatures through three modes — each thing one to itself, one creature united to another, the whole universe one with its Maker — and derives all three from the single and simple unity whose source is that One which is both first one and at the same time three and one: the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit [[wallis1965-heptaplus:108]]. This passage shows the Platonic-Dionysian metaphysics of the one working inside Christian trinitarian theology; whether the unity arrived at is the one of the first chapter of *De ente*, above being, or the one of the second chapter, God in himself as cause, or both at once, is a question the texts raise without settling (ed.).

Several threads remain open (ed.). The most basic is the one Miller identifies in the text: in chapter 9, God considered in himself is first the plenitude of existence and then one, whereas in the reversed sequence as cause one is first; Pico himself notes that these points remain full of many and great questions [[wallis1965-deente:091]]. Howlett's observation that the two arguments of chapter 5 apparently contradict each other adds a second layer: the argument that God is existence itself, in which case being is his most proper predicate, seems to conflict with the argument that God is above being as the Plotinian One or the Ein-Sof [[howlett2021:132]]. Whether *De ente et uno* reconciles these tensions or marks the place where the concordance project reaches its limit is not answered by the text as it survives, which breaks off before the promised treatment of the *Sophist* [[wallis1965-deente:091|edelheit2022:006]].
