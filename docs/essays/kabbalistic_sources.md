# Pico's Two Kabbalot: Sources, Translators, and the Programme of the Conclusiones

This essay has five stops: the two distinct Kabbalistic traditions Pico brought to the Conclusiones; the evidence for which specific texts he demonstrably used; the role of Flavius Mithridates as translator and the problem of Christianizing interpolations; the Kabbalistic technique in the Heptaplus; and the condemned magical thesis and the distinction Pico drew between his two Kabbalot in the Apologia (ed.).

## Stop 1 — The two tracks: sefirot and letters

Wirszubski draws a foundational distinction between the two Kabbalistic traditions Pico worked with (ed.). The sefirotic track, represented by Menahem Recanati, is mainly concerned with the ten sefirot as divine emanations and uses symbolical exegesis of Scripture; the letter track, represented by Abraham Abulafia, is concerned with the name or names of God and uses combinations of letters as a discipline for union with God [[wirszubski1989-angels:078]]. Wirszubski notes that many Kabbalists conceived the three triads of sefirot as representing the three stages of being, Intellect, Soul and Nature, citing Azriel's *Quaestiones super Decem Numerationibus* as the locus classicus [[wirszubski1989-angels:007]]. Wirszubski says Pico's sixty-sixth Kabbalistic thesis maps the parts of the human soul onto the ten sefirot, and that the ten avengers within us in the preceding thesis are its counterpart [[wirszubski1989-angels:041]]. Wirszubski further explains the tenth thesis of the same series, *mala coordinatio denaria*, as the evil or unholy counterpart of the hierarchy of the ten sefirot, noting that it would be risky to draw conclusions about Pico's own view of evil from a thesis that is not offered *secundum opinionem propriam* [[wirszubski1989-angels:040]].

## Stop 2 — The source question: which texts Pico actually used

Wirszubski argues that the inventory of Pico's library, the authors Pico mentions, and even the translations made for him may all mislead as evidence, and that the only evidence that can be absolutely trusted for which Kabbalistic texts Pico used is the internal evidence of the theses themselves, by derivation of texts [[wirszubski1989-angels:072]]. Applying that method, Wirszubski identifies the likeliest direct source of thesis II — on the nine angelic hierarchies with the form Aralim — as Abraham Axelrad's *Corona Nominis Boni*, found in a Vatican Hebrew codex, on the ground of the unusual number nine and the unusual form Aralim [[wirszubski1989-angels:001]]. Wirszubski judges that the ultimate source of the thesis that no angel having six wings is ever transformed is undoubtedly the Zohar, but that there is no need to look beyond Recanati, who quotes it, for the immediate source [[wirszubski1989-angels:113]]. For the Abulafian track, Wirszubski argues that *De Secretis Legis* is the only book Pico is known to have read before the Apologia that couples divine names and the combination of letters with the identification of Metatron, and concludes that Abulafia is the obvious and only candidate for the intervening source between the Talmud and Maimonides, on the one hand, and Pico's picture of Kabbalah on the other [[wirszubski1989-angels:130]].

Wirszubski also establishes several negative results (ed.). He notes that none of Pico's Kabbalistic theses from the fifth to the seventy-second presupposes the divisions and definitions of the first four, and allows that the first four were added late, after most of the others had been written [[wirszubski1989-angels:017]]. There is no conclusive evidence that Pico read Nahmanides before completing the theses: the earliest explicit reference appears in the Heptaplus, written about two years after the Conclusiones, after his return from France in 1488 [[wirszubski1989-angels:092]]. Black concludes, given the limits of the evidence, that one cannot assume Pico could read complete Kabbalistic treatises unaided even in 1489; he was a pioneer in his area of interest rather than in linguistic ability, and approached it through the translated corpus and oral contact [[black2006-a:052]].

## Stop 3 — Mithridates, translations, and interpolations

Ficino wrote to Pico on 8 September 1486 asking for the return of his Latin Koran, and Pico's undated reply from La Fratta, not earlier than the middle of October 1486, says he had devoted a month to Hebrew and then given himself entirely to other languages [[wirszubski1989-angels:100]]. Borghesi reports from one of Ficino's letters that Elia had debated in Pico's house with the convert Mithridates, and that Mithridates soon began teaching Hebrew to Pico [[dougherty2008:065]]. The relationship was productive but also treacherous for a reader of the translations (ed.). Wirszubski argues from Mithridates's own notes that the twelve-book *De Anima* attributed to Eleazar of Worms was written when Pico was already in Rome and after the consequences of the publication of the Conclusiones, so that Pico could not have read it before completing his theses [[wirszubski1989-angels:108]]. Wirszubski reports that Mithridates's notes identify the figure called Malfecta in those notes as Giovanni Battista Cibo, Cardinal of Melfi and Mithridates's former patron, who as Innocent VIII later succeeded Sixtus IV — establishing a personal connection between the translator and the pope who condemned thirteen of Pico's theses [[wirszubski1989-angels:110]].

The problem of interpolation runs through Wirszubski's analysis (ed.). On Mithridates's translation of a passage from Moses ben Nahman's preface embedded in Abulafia's preface, Wirszubski notes that the statement that there are four names is an unnecessary addition by Mithridates, since neither Moses ben Nahman nor Abulafia says four names and the obvious division is into three; italics in the manuscript mark interpolations [[wirszubski1989-angels:131]]. Wirszubski says the Latin translation of the source underlying the twentieth Kabbalistic thesis adds a Trinitarian clause that the Hebrew lacks, connecting the three superior sefirot with the Trinity, and that the effect of this interpolation can be seen directly in Pico's twentieth Kabbalistic thesis [[wirszubski1989-angels:120]]. Wirszubski hedges that not all Christianizing interpolations can be attributed to Mithridates with certainty: authorship of each must be established independently, and at the present state of research this cannot be done for all of them [[wirszubski1989-angels:122]].

## Stop 4 — The Heptaplus and the letter-combination technique

By the time Pico wrote the Heptaplus, roughly two years after the Conclusiones, the Kabbalistic learning was applied with more structural confidence (ed.). Black summarises the doctrine that Moses passed through forty-nine of the fifty gates of understanding on Sinai, acquiring knowledge of all created entities, and proposes that the Heptaplus is a guide to how that knowledge was concealed in Genesis, reflecting the gates in content and structure [[black2006-c:110]]. Pico says that among the doctrines of ancient Hebrew learning the six days denote the six thousand years of the world, a view confirmed by Moses of Gerona and mentioned by Jerome, and that his interpretation will furnish weapons against the stony hearts of the Hebrews drawn from their own arsenals [[wallis1965-heptaplus:124]]. Wirszubski reads the ninth and tenth Orphic theses, which pair the Curetes of Orpheus with the *potestates* of Dionysius, as requiring an *opus Cabalae* related to the Fear of Isaac, which in the language of Kabbalah denotes the fifth sefirah, Might or Judgment, so that the Kabbalistic work joined to Orphic magic involves sefirot even though the precise modus operandi is unknown [[wirszubski1989-angels:039]].

The final section of the Heptaplus marks the most explicit convergence between Kabbalistic technique and biblical exegesis (ed.). Black says that section abandons allegory for a technique of letter combination derived from Kabbalistic exegetical works, producing a sentence that summarises the Heptaplus and confirms Pico's claim that everything can be found in Moses's text [[black2006-a:100]]. Pico in the Second Exposition reports that Isaac the philosopher takes the tenth sphere to be what Ezekiel called the sapphire in the likeness of a throne, its immobility being signified by the likeness to a throne [[wallis1965-heptaplus:044]].

## Stop 5 — The condemned thesis and the two Kabbalot

The condemned magical thesis provides the clearest window into what Pico thought he was doing with Kabbalah (ed.). Wirszubski reports that none of the thirteen condemned theses is itself a Kabbalistic thesis, and that the Oration was meant as the opening address of the public debate on the nine hundred theses but was not delivered; Kabbalah enters the condemned material through the magical thesis that no science certifies Christ's divinity better than magic and Kabbalah [[wirszubski1989-angels:128]]. Wirszubski says Pico's defence of that thesis rests entirely on a distinction between revealed and nonrevealed science, and that the line of defence requires distinguishing the Kabbalah that is the foundation of the Conclusiones Cabalisticae from the Kabbalah involved in the condemned magical thesis — two Kabbalot operating under different epistemic conditions [[wirszubski1989-angels:129]]. Garsia's attack, as Edelheit reports, insists that magic and Kabbalah cannot certify Christ's divinity better than theology, and that Pico's thesis, by implying they can replace faith, is false and heretical; Edelheit reads in the theme an anticipation of the dispute over true and false prophecy in 1490s Florence [[edelheit2022:146]]. Copenhaver observes that Pico's projects for philosophical concord and for a Kabbalist Christianity were distinctly his own innovations, contrasting with the settled dogmas of incarnation and eucharist that his theological adventuring provoked the authorities to defend [[copenhaver2022-angels:045]].

Copenhaver reports that although the Oration shows little about Kabbalah as such, comparison with the Conclusiones reveals it to be abundantly Kabbalist: magic and Kabbalah help mortals achieve union with God, with Kabbalah at a higher level helping humans change into angels and merge with God [[copenhaver2019-angels:070]]. Howlett, engaging with Idel's argument that both Pico and Alemanno believed the soul can cleave to the Ein-Sof, says she sees no evidence of this in Pico; his language, she holds, suggests the soul cleaves to Keter, the first sefirah, not to the undifferentiated infinite behind it [[howlett2021:206]]. Wirszubski says that next to Flavius Mithridates the person who matters most for Pico's encounter with Kabbalah is Johanan Alemanno, identified in a letter as Pico's teacher, and that it remains unknown what Johanan taught Pico and when their association began, though it is usually dated to 1488 in Florence — after the Conclusiones, after the condemnation, and after Pico had already committed to the two-track programme [[wirszubski1989-angels:125]].
