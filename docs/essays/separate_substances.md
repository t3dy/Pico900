# Pure Intellect and the Question of Place: Pico on Separate Substances

This essay has five stops: the three-world framework of the *Heptaplus* and its philosophical sources; the angelic nature as pure intellect with its double imperfection; the relationship between angels and humans and the faculty through which they share; the condemned thesis on angel location and Pico's defence; and the treatment of separate substances in the Conclusiones through the Thomist and Aegidian theses (ed.).

## Stop 1 — The three worlds and the nine orders

Black argues that the model of the *Heptaplus* is in accordance with Aristotle's ontology at the start of *Metaphysics* XII, of sensible perishable, sensible eternal and immovable substance, matching Pico's sublunar, celestial and intellectual worlds, though he considers a direct link implausible without later developments in the Aristotelian tradition [[black2006-b:100]]. In the Second Exposition Pico notes that "thus far the heavens have been treated as luminous bodies and that nothing has been said by the Prophet of their intelligence or of their motive force, an order which the *Timaeus* also follows, first constructing the body and then adding the soul to the completed body" [[wallis1965-heptaplus:050]]. The intellectual world — the third level above the sublunar and celestial — is the level of separate and immovable substances (ed.).

In the Third Exposition Pico says the Prophet refutes the error of the philosophers "who believed that there are certain princely intellectual substances but denied that each of them is set over a numerous multitude of lower rank, like the commander of a legion, as the theologians say," and proposes that "since we know of nine orders of angels, and since each order has chosen its own leader by lot, let us fashion that leader and prince in our minds as a great sphere, and then the following army as the inhabitants and ornament of that sphere" [[wallis1965-heptaplus:083]]. Edelheit notes that Ficino had already claimed to have found that whatever Numenius, Philo, Plotinus, Iamblichus and Proclus said about the divine mind and the angels was taken from John, Paul, Hierotheus and Dionysius the Areopagite [[edelheit2008:036]]. The *Heptaplus*'s Third Exposition is a deployment of that Dionysian inheritance in the service of biblical exegesis (ed.). The editors of the Borghesi *Oration* note that the order of angels, stars, and lower creatures in section 6 is repeated in reverse order in section 11 [[borghesi2012:042]], and in section 6 Pico says man received a condition to be envied "not only by beasts but even by the stars and the intelligences dwelling beyond this world" [[borghesi2012:003]].

## Stop 2 — The angelic nature: pure intellect and double imperfection

In the Third Exposition chapter 2 Pico says that "light was made in the angel, the light of intelligible forms," and gives as the reason that "intelligible species are united to angelic minds by eternal links and an indivisible bond, not a vague and customary one as happens with the human intellect" [[wallis1965-heptaplus:072]]. Black reports that Dionysius and Thomas Aquinas, as Pico reads them, both hold that angelic intelligence is superior to human because it comprehends with fewer forms, and that in the *Heptaplus* intelligible forms are accidental to the angel's intellect yet bound to it indivisibly and perpetually, whereas the bond to the human intellect is vague and ordinary [[black2006-b:127]]; Black adds that Pico contrasts the human mind's need for many forms with the angelic mind's need for only a few, connecting forms with words and using the contrast to express the uniqueness of Moses's text [[black2006-c:051]].

In the Fifth Exposition Pico writes that "to speak of the angelic nature, which is pure intellect, let us first imagine that minds are like eyes," and that "intellects are eyes, intelligible truth is light, and the intellect, itself intelligible, has a kind of inner light by which it can see itself but not other things; it needs the forms and ideas of things, like rays of invisible light, for the intelligible truth to be clearly discerned" [[wallis1965-heptaplus:091]]. Howlett quotes Pico's *Heptaplus* that the angel carries a double imperfection: "the one, that it is not being itself but only an essence to which being comes by participation, so that it may be; the other, that it is not intelligence itself but only happens to understand, since by its nature it is an intellect capable of understanding" [[howlett2021:166]]. *De ente et uno* chapter 5 draws the same consequence in metaphysical terms: "the life of the angels is not perfect; unless the vivifying ray of divine light constantly warmed it, it would all fall into nothingness" [[wallis1965-deente:043]]. Allen cites Ficino's formulation of the same dependence: divine light poured into the angel becomes an intellectual light understood intuitively, while when poured into the soul it becomes rational light understood intuitively and also discursively [[allen2017-other:044]].

## Stop 3 — Angels and humans: the shared faculty

In the Fourth Exposition Pico says that "since we share no less with the angels than with the brutes, just as below reason there are the senses through which we have fellowship with the animals, so above reason is the intelligence through which we are able to say with John, 'our fellowship is with angels'" [[wallis1965-heptaplus:086]]. Black identifies the Spirit of God that moves over the upper waters in the Fourth Exposition with the greater and more divine intellect that illuminates the human intellect, and notes that Pico leaves open whether this is God himself or a mind close and akin to man [[black2006-a:081]]. The limit of what natural philosophy can say about that illuminating intellect is precisely what the Seventh Exposition marks (ed.): Pico says the philosophers ascribe to the angels no further knowledge of God than that by which they know themselves, so that the angels understand God only as his nature is made manifest in their own substance [[wallis1965-heptaplus:118]].

The asymmetry between human and angelic existence is drawn in the Oration (ed.). Pico says the highest Intelligences "have been, either from the beginning or soon thereafter, what they are perpetually going to be throughout eternity," while man is given the power to become what he wills [[borghesi2012:006]]; the intelligences beyond this world are said to envy the human condition precisely because man is not fixed in a nature as they are [[borghesi2012:003]]. Allen cites the Commento's qualification that keeps this from collapsing the angel into the Son of the theologians: the son of God spoken of by the Platonists is to be compared to the first and noblest angel created by God, and not to the Son of the theologians [[allen2017-other:017]].

## Stop 4 — The condemned thesis: angel location and Pico's defence

The first of the thirteen condemned theses concerned the descent of Christ into hell, and its philosophical nerve was the question of how a disembodied soul can be in a place at all (ed.). Copenhaver paraphrases the thesis: Christ did not truly, as far as real presence is concerned, descend to Hell as proposed by Thomas and on the usual approach, but only in regard to the effect; and Pico identifies the usual approach with that of Scotus, Bonaventura, the Scotists and almost all nominalists, believing Thomas also holds it [[copenhaver2022-angels:003]]. Edelheit summarises Pico's own opinion: that only Christ's soul descended into hell, leading immediately to the question of how separate souls, immaterial substances or angels stand in relation to a physical place, with presence identified with operation [[edelheit2022:078]]; the first explanation given in the defence, resting largely on Gregory of Nyssa, concludes that Christ's soul was neither inside nor outside hell but truly nowhere, and was said to be in hell because it operated there [[edelheit2008:077]].

Garsia, the theologian who attacked the thesis in the *Determinationes magistrales*, classified five views of angelic place (ed.). Edelheit reconstructs them: Scotists and Nominalists hold definitive presence through the angel's own nature; a second view attributed to the school's founder limits power rather than essence; Thomists identify operation as the reason for the angel's being in a place; a fourth view assigns presence to effect alone; and the fifth view, which Garsia says Pico follows, holds that the angel is nowhere and not moved locally by its substance or by operation [[edelheit2022:128]]. Edelheit shows that Garsia also separates the philosophers, obliged by Aristotelian astronomy and metaphysics to regard intelligences as completely immovable and without attachment to a place, from some theologians who hold as a probable opinion that angels and separate souls are located and move locally in themselves and in their proper substance [[edelheit2022:127]]. Copenhaver observes that Pico added the qualification about the usual approach to the Q1 thesis because he disagreed to some extent not just with Aquinas but also with Scotus and the others, and cited an uncondemned thesis that Thomas and Scotus do not differ about angels on their manner of being in a place [[copenhaver2022-angels:004]].

Copenhaver traces a precedent in Godfrey of Fontaines for treating the question as open (ed.). In a quodlibetal question on angels, Godfrey argued that where an issue is so unsettled that beliefs may vary without risk to faith and morals, chaining people to one view blocks progress; Copenhaver reads this as debate open in the scholastic way, where fides always comes first in the fides quaerens intellectum sequence, not a call to enlightenment [[copenhaver2022-angels:011]]. Copenhaver poses the heart of Pico's defence as the question of what a believer is obliged to believe about the descent, given the puzzle of how a disembodied soul goes to a place [[copenhaver2022-angels:029]].

## Stop 5 — Separate substances in the Conclusiones

The Conclusiones engage the metaphysics of separate substances through several scholastic lineages (ed.). Edelheit says Pico's thesis 31 — that essence and existence in any created being are in fact distinguished — concerns the distinction Thomas discusses in *De ente et essentia*, where in God essence and existence are identical while in created intellectual substances existence is limited and finite, dependent on the ability of a receiving nature, though their quiddity is absolute and contains no matter [[edelheit2022:072]]. Pico carries the same distinction into his reply to Cittadini: while everything around God that is actual is a composite of essence and existence, God alone is pure existence from which all created existence derives [[edelheit2022:031]]; Cittadini counters that if existence and essence are really distinct and the essence of an idea is God, then the existence of that idea would differ from God, giving a real composition in God [[edelheit2022:051]].

Edelheit identifies thesis 22 — that God is not seen through an outward appearance or the sense of sight but is connected to the intellect through his essence as an intelligible species — as drawing on Thomas's commentary on the Sentences, where Thomas distinguishes the philosophers, who take the ultimate happiness of mankind to consist in understanding substances separated from matter, from the theologians, who hold that the human intellect can attain to seeing the divine essence [[edelheit2022:066]]. On thesis 40, that difficulty in understanding can originate partly in the intellect itself and partly in the intelligible object, Edelheit notes that Thomas's *Summa contra gentiles* 3, 45 addresses the same question but stresses the limitations of human understanding in this life and the inaccessibility of separated substances specifically; Pico seems to treat it as a more general epistemological question without committing himself to separated substances only [[edelheit2022:074]]. On the Aegidian conclusions, Edelheit reports that Pico's thesis 5 — that angels were not created in grace — derives from Giles's commentary on the Sentences, while the *Reportatio* gives a more complicated view opposed to Pico's thesis, where the good angels reach beatitude through grace with which they were created [[edelheit2022:106]]. Allen reports Ficino, in an account of the Averroist argument, adducing on Thomist grounds that there are as many angelic species as there are individual angels, so that not many angels exist in one angelic species but rather as many species of angels as there are individuals [[allen2017-other:063]].

Black notes that the tradition of commentary on the active and potential intellects reached no consensus on whether the active intellect is God, a transcendent entity between God and man, or an aspect of the individual human soul [[black2006-c:001]]; this unresolved question runs beneath the entire complex, connecting the *Heptaplus*'s open identification of the illuminating intellect as either God or a proximate mind with thesis 2K10's Metatron equivalences and with Pico's refusal to bind the angel-location question to any one scholastic position (ed.).
