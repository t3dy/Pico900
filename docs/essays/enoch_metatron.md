# Metatron and the Blank: Enoch, the Angel Intellect, and Pico's Secret Theology

This essay has five stops (ed.). The first examines a single blank space and its three competing readings across the printed tradition and the manuscript record. The second asks what Metatron is — who and what the name designates in the Kabbalistic literature Pico read — and what is meant when the sources call him the agent intellect. The third follows Metatron into the Conclusiones, where thesis 2K10 maps him onto the ancient theologians of the Gentiles, and reads the scholarly argument about which Hebrew word belonged in that thesis's own blank. The fourth tracks Metatron through the Oration, where he anchors a programme of ascent from human to angelic existence. The fifth names the theological risks the Oration brings to the surface by invoking this figure, and closes with the open questions the sources leave unresolved.

## Stop 1 — The blank and its three readings

The Oration's thirty-fifth section says that "even the most secret Hebrew theology at one time transforms holy Enoch into an angel of divinity," naming the angel in Hebrew and adding that "at other times it reshapes other men into other spirits" [[borghesi2012:009]]. The Hebrew name of that angel is what is in dispute (ed.). In the manuscript tradition the name appears in Hebrew characters; in the printed tradition, it does not (ed.).

The three readings reflect three moments in the transmission (ed.). The editio princeps, the first printed edition, leaves a blank space where the Hebrew word should be [[borghesi2012:054]]. The two Basel editions of Pico's Opera Omnia (1557 and 1572) supply instead a Hebrew retroversion of the Latin angelum divinitatis, specifically *Mal'akh ha-Sekinach* [[borghesi2012:054]]. The manuscript Palatinus 885, Biblioteca Nazionale Centrale di Firenze, preserves Hebrew characters that read as Metatron [[wirszubski1989-angels:035]]. Bausi's modern critical edition, following Wirszubski, restores those characters from the manuscript, reading them as Metatron; the editors' notes report that the manuscript may represent one of two drafts Pico prepared in the autumn of 1486 before moving to Rome [[borghesi2012:055]].

Wirszubski's reason for preferring the manuscript is not simply that it is the older witness (ed.). He argues that its reading is warranted by Pico's own Commento, which mentions Metatron independently: in Wirszubski's account of the Commento, Pico says that the Kabbalist sages declare that Enoch is transformed into Matatron, the angel of divinity, and that other men are sometimes transformed into other spirits [[wirszubski1989-angels:034]]. This parallel lets Wirszubski treat the Oration's manuscript reading and the Commento's Italian passage as mutually confirming, rather than the manuscript reading as an isolated conjecture [[wirszubski1989-angels:036]]. Copenhaver translates: "For with their more secret theology, Hebrews too sometimes transform the blessed Enoch into an angel of divinity, whom they call מטטרון, and sometimes they change others into other divine powers" — it is spiritual intelligence, not removing the body, that makes an angel [[copenhaver2019-angels:077]].

The Oration at this point has said nothing yet about Kabbalah; the name arrives with a mention of "more secret theology" and nothing else [[copenhaver2019-angels:004]]. Christian readers of the printed editions would have found the blank, or the Basel editors' back-translation, in place of the name itself. Whether they could have understood it, had the name stood there, is another matter (ed.).

## Stop 2 — Metatron in the Kabbalistic tradition

Metatron is not a straightforwardly Kabbalistic figure: he belongs first to a wider Jewish angelological tradition, and only some of his roles are distinctively Kabbalistic (ed.). Howlett's survey of the sources reports that Enoch is synonymous with Metatron, who is described as a high angelic power or the voice of God, and that by the middle of the thirteenth century the tradition had distinguished a higher, Great Metatron from a lower one [[howlett2021:096]]. Wirszubski says that Jewish angelology has an angel who could be called son of God, wisdom, mind and intelligible sphere, and identifies him as Metatron; he notes that Metatron appears twice in Pico's writings, both from about the time of the Conclusiones [[wirszubski1989-angels:033]].

His most technically specified role in the literature Pico read is as the agent intellect (ed.). Wirszubski prints a Latin passage — the sixth secret on the angel and the intellect — which identifies Mattatron, prince of the face, with the separate intellect that brings our intellect from potency to act, gives his proper name as the same as his master's, and reports that the sages call him Henoch, identifying him as Mattatron [[wirszubski1989-angels:049]]. Howlett cites Abulafia's commentary on Maimonides's Guide for the same identification, where the Metatron is described as "the thing that is actualizing our intellect from its potentiality," "itself an intellect detached from any materiality," the prince of the world and the angel of the Face, "the universal spirit, which has been called by the philosophers the Agent Intellect" [[howlett2021:163]]. A similar formulation appears in a text Mithridates translated for Pico: Abulafia writes that "what takes our intellect from potency to act is an Intellect separated from all matter," called the Prince of the World and Mattatron, Prince of the Faces [[copenhaver2019-angels:006]].

The Liber Redemptionis, also in a Mithridates translation Pico possessed, combines the rationalistic philosophy of Maimonides's Guide with the combinatory letter method of the Sefer Yesirah, and contains the doctrine of the Active Intellect as the prince of the divine presence whose name is like the name of his master, along with everything this identification implies about the actualization and union of the human intellect with the active one [[wirszubski1989-angels:043]]. The passage in the preface to the Kabbalistic demonstrations, as Wirszubski prints it, names the archangel prefect over the hierarchy of angels called hisim as the intellectus agens, "sive Mattatron" [[wirszubski1989-angels:047]]; Wirszubski notes, however, that some of the words in the Latin — the identification of the Agent Intellect with Metatron, or the doctrine of the coincidence of opposites — are italicized in the manuscript as having no counterpart in the Hebrew original, interpolated by someone, perhaps but not certainly Mithridates [[wirszubski1989-angels:047]].

Howlett adds a further identification from Maimonides, mediated through Abulafia: the ultimate aim of the ascent to the divine is the Active Intellect itself, expressed as Hokmah but also as Enoch, Metatron and the Cherubic realm [[howlett2021:086]]. In Howlett's account, this identification reaches Pico through a chain — Maimonides on prophecy and the Active Intellect, Abulafia applying it to Metatron — that sits on the border between rationalist philosophy and Kabbalistic mysticism, combining Neo-Aristotelian epistemology with Ashkenazi esotericism [[howlett2021:163]].

## Stop 3 — Metatron in the Conclusiones: the tenth Kabbalistic thesis

Metatron appears in the Conclusiones explicitly in two theses (ed.). Thesis 2K11 states that the way in which rational souls are sacrificed to God by an archangel happens only by the soul's parting from the body, not the body's parting from the soul [[copenhaver2019-angels:094]]. Thesis 2K10 presents the more contested formulation: its printed text has a blank in the place of a Hebrew name, and the claim the thesis makes is that what Kabbalists call that name "is beyond doubt what Orpheus names Pallas, Zoroaster the Father's Mind, Mercury God's Son, Pythagoras Wisdom, Parmenides the Intelligible Sphere" [[copenhaver2019-angels:094]]. Howlett quotes the same passage: "That which among the Cabalists is called *Metatron* is without doubt that which is called Pallas by Orpheus, the paternal mind by Zoroaster, the son of God by Hermes, wisdom by Pythagoras, the intelligible sphere by Parmenides" [[howlett2021:223]].

The blank in the printed Conclusiones is the same problem as the blank in the printed Oration, but here the argument from the external witness does not settle it (ed.). Francesco Giorgio, the Franciscan Kabbalist, later read the blank as Hokmah, the second sefirah [[wirszubski1989-angels:031]]. Wirszubski argues instead for Metatron [[wirszubski1989-angels:037]]. His case turns on the synonyms the thesis supplies: Metatron is sometimes identified with the Active Intellect and is also called son, and those identifications match the thesis's list of equivalents — Pallas, wisdom, the intelligible sphere, the Father's mind, God's son — where Hokmah alone would not supply the son-of-God equivalence [[wirszubski1989-angels:037]]. Copenhaver sides with Wirszubski, because the Agent Intellect — explicitly identified with Metatron by another thesis that uses the name in Latin — is a better match for the other entities named in the thesis than Hokmah is [[copenhaver2019-angels:097]]. Ebgi, following Zorzi's commentary, has argued for Hokmah; the dispute between his reading and Wirszubski's is recorded and remains open [[copenhaver2019-angels:097]].

Wirszubski draws a sharper implication from Giorgio's reading: if the blank is Hokmah, and if Hokmah as used in Pico's Kabbalistic symbolic language stands for Jesus Christ — as it does in three other theses (the thirty-seventh, forty-second, and thirty-ninth Kabbalistic conclusions) — then the thesis would identify the Orphic Pallas, the Hermetic son of God, the Zoroastrian paternal mind and the Pythagorean wisdom directly with Jesus Christ [[wirszubski1989-angels:031]]. Pico's Commento, in Wirszubski's account of it, warns against precisely that identification, saying that what the Hermeticists call the son of God ought not to be identified with the Christian Son of God [[wirszubski1989-angels:031]]. The dilemma follows: either Pico is inconsistent between the Conclusiones and the Commento, or Giorgio's reading is not what Pico intended [[wirszubski1989-angels:031]].

Wirszubski's thesis is that Pico deployed Metatron, not Hokmah, at this point for a specific double purpose: to show correspondences between the Kabbala and the ancient theology of the Gentiles, and at the same time to draw a dividing line between Christianity and that ancient theology [[wirszubski1989-angels:037]]. The Metatron of the Kabbalists could be equated with the figures of multiple traditions without those traditions' images being pulled inside the Christian theological sequence [[wirszubski1989-angels:037]]. Hokmah, in Pico's own symbolic alphabet, could not do that work without implicating Christ [[wirszubski1989-angels:031]].

A third instance of Metatron in the Conclusiones is more elliptical (ed.). The thirteenth thesis on the sayings of Zoroaster links *puer* (youth) to *intellectus*. Wirszubski judges it likely that the connecting link is Metatron, who is called *na'ar* — youth — in the sources and is sometimes identified with the Active Intellect, though he admits he does not know in what context the interpreters of Zoroaster used *puer* [[wirszubski1989-angels:029]].

## Stop 4 — Metatron in the Oration: ascent and angel magic

Pico's Oration names Metatron explicitly once, in the passage on the transformation of Enoch discussed above, but uses him more extensively as the implied goal and pattern of the ascent the speech recommends (ed.). Copenhaver's reading of the Oration treats the speech as an exhortation to angel magic: Pico wants humans to become angels and then to be annihilated in God, and the Oration as a whole tells them how [[copenhaver2019-angels:067]]. In Copenhaver's reading, the peace that God makes on high, the descent of angels so that humans ascending might be made into angels, is this angel magic, a theurgy to emulate Enoch's transformation into Metatron — "a joyous surrender of human personality when all traces of the individual dissolve in God's supernal peace" [[copenhaver2019-angels:037]].

The named angels in the Oration — Gabriel, Metatron, Michael, Raphael — appear as monitors of the Cherubic life [[copenhaver2019-angels:051]]. Abulafia's own teaching, as Copenhaver quotes it, called Cherub, Enoch and Metatron different names in Hebrew for the Agent Intellect, who had "many other names besides" and "rules over the hierarchy of angels" [[copenhaver2019-angels:051]]. The order of Pallas — which the Oration calls the knot of primary minds, protector of contemplative philosophy — which the human ascender must first emulate before being carried toward the heights of love [[copenhaver2019-angels:081]], is connected by thesis 2K10 to Metatron, so that the Cherub's knot — as Copenhaver argues — is ultimately tied by Pallas Athena as Metatron within the Seraphic fire surrounding God [[copenhaver2019-angels:021]].

Howlett develops the ascent in terms of a double intellectual sonship drawn from Idel's reading of the Kabbalistic sources (ed.). The Metatron can also be understood as the ascended Enoch, the apotheosis of the seeker who has pursued God, so that the mystic's transformation into Metatron establishes "a double intellectual sonship: that of the cosmic, who is the Agent Intellect, and an angel and son... and that of the mystic, who became a son by becoming an intellect in actu" [[howlett2021:164]]. The Oration's own language about the intellectual man who can become an angel and son of God is read by Howlett, following Idel, as reflecting this transformative understanding [[howlett2021:164]]. In Howlett's account of the Commento, the angelic mind is the home of the Metatron, the transfigured Enoch, who prefigures Christ in the Old Testament; the angelic mind can also be called the son of God, first because Christ is present above it, and second because it is the most perfect effect God can produce [[howlett2021:162]].

Abulafia urged his followers to turn themselves into separate angels by attaining prophetic ecstasy, to be "saved by this from natural death"; his rites included rites to join with Metatron, the angel prince he identified with the Agent Intellect [[copenhaver2019-angels:054]]. On Abulafia's conception, Copenhaver notes, mystical union with Metatron eradicates the self, and the mystic's role is messianic as well as prophetic — the dissolution of the human self in the Godhead is not only personal liberation but service to others [[copenhaver2019-angels:054]]. Allen's reading of the Commento passage on the death of the soul follows the same path: to unite with intelligible Beauty means, in Allen's account of the Commento, to pass "from human existence to intellectual existence," transformed by that death into an angel, as "the Cabalists declare Enoch was transformed into Metatron, 'the angel of divinity'" [[allen2017-venus:013]].

## Stop 5 — The theological risks and what remains open

The Metatron speculation gave Pico evidence, as Copenhaver reports, for more than one Trinity among the sefirot (ed.). Shekinah, Shaddai and Holy Spirit were other names for Metatron in the Abulafian tradition; addressing Metatron as Shaddai rested on the numerological equivalence of both names at the value 314 [[copenhaver2019-angels:055]]. That Metatron could be addressed as Shaddai, a name belonging to God, reached into theological territory a Christian could not enter without permission, and Copenhaver observes that the speculations about Metatron "also reached into places that good Christians like Pico should have approached only with rites sanctioned by the Church" [[copenhaver2019-angels:056]].

Copenhaver raises the question whether Pico deliberately kept angel magic quiet while advertising natural magic in the Conclusiones and the Oration: from a prayer addressed in church to Saint Michael it was, as Copenhaver puts it, "a short step to a liturgy for Metatron, a secret but arguably lawful magic invoking a good personal agent with superhuman powers" [[copenhaver2019-angels:050]]. The magic theses of T2, on this reading, suggest a deliberate silence about the full scope of what Pico was recommending [[copenhaver2019-angels:050]].

Several questions the preceding stops have touched recur here as genuinely unsettled (ed.). The most basic textual question — which Hebrew word Pico wrote in the blank of thesis 2K10, Metatron or Hokmah — is unresolved; Wirszubski and Copenhaver argue for Metatron, Ebgi and Giorgio's commentary for Hokmah, and the argument turns on theological commitments the thesis itself does not make explicit [[copenhaver2019-angels:097|wirszubski1989-angels:031]]. The editorial question of which words in the Mithridates Latin translations are Abulafia's and which are the translator's interpolations is unresolved; in the passage that identifies the archangel prefect of the hisim with the agent intellect and with Mattatron, Wirszubski finds some italicized words that have no Hebrew counterpart, but without knowing which they are it is not clear whether the identification of Metatron with the Agent Intellect was already in Abulafia's text or was added in translation [[wirszubski1989-angels:047|wirszubski1989-angels:050]]. Whether the closing part of the De Secretis Legis passage is by Abulafia or by an interpolator — possibly but not certainly Mithridates — is also unresolved [[wirszubski1989-angels:050]].

A further open question has to do with the relationship between Metatron as a figure of the ascent and Metatron as the dividing line from Christianity (ed.). Wirszubski's argument is that Pico used the figure to make correspondences between traditions and simultaneously to ensure those correspondences did not reach all the way into the Christian sequence [[wirszubski1989-angels:037]]. Howlett's reading, following the double sonship, moves in the opposite direction: for her, the transformative reading of Metatron as the ascended Enoch implies that the mystic's becoming an intellect in act, a son by actualized intellect, is a mode of participation in what the Commento calls the angelic mind, which in turn can prefigure Christ [[howlett2021:164|howlett2021:162]]. Whether the dividing line Wirszubski locates in the Conclusiones is consistent with the porosity toward Christology that Howlett finds in the ascent programme is a question the sources raise without resolving (ed.).
