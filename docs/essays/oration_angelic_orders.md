# The Ladder and the Bosom: Angelic Ascent in Pico's Oration

This essay has five stops: the three Dionysian orders as the framework of the Oration's opening; man's place in the world as the condition of possibility for becoming; Jacob's ladder and the three angelic guides, Raphael, Gabriel and Michael; the Kabbalistic dimension of those three guides; and the limit at the top of the ladder and what cannot be attained by ascending (ed.).

## Stop 1 — The three orders and the Dionysian activities

The editors of the Borghesi *Oration* note that the word *dignity* appears for the first time in the speech at the passage on emulating the dignity and glory of the Seraphim, Cherubim and Thrones [[borghesi2012:067]]. In the Third Exposition of the *Heptaplus* Pico cites Dionysius for the distribution of the three angelic activities: purification, illumination and perfection are distributed so that the lowest order purifies, the highest perfects, and the middle order illuminates; the supercelestial ones perfect the purified with a fiery and life-giving dew [[wallis1965-heptaplus:081]]. Black says Pico takes the Cherubim as the exemplar for human life in the Oration, and reads their Dionysian threefold process — cleansed, illuminated, perfected — as a method of intellectual ascent [[black2006-c:011]]. Copenhaver describes Dionysius's practical theology, whose forms the Oration inherits, as ascetic, ecstatic, esoteric and theurgic, and as otherworldly as the Cherubic life Pico advocated [[copenhaver2019-angels:030]].

## Stop 2 — Man's place and the possibility of becoming

Pico says that when man was to be made every place in the world was already filled and had been assigned to the highest, the middle and the lowest orders [[borghesi2012:005]]; the result is that man has no fixed seat, which Pico reads not as privation but as the condition of all becoming (ed.). Pico says that a man who cultivates his intellectual seeds will be an angel and a son of God, just as cultivating the rational seeds makes him a heavenly being [[borghesi2012:007]]. What makes an angel, Pico says, is spiritual intelligence and not separation from the body [[borghesi2012:010]]. Allen judges that the most significant difference between Pico and Ficino lies in Pico's dual Neoplatonic and anthropocentric orientation, which fastens attention on the Idea of Man almost exclusively [[allen2017-other:031]]. Sudduth's essay in the Dougherty volume reports Pico's three worlds — the angelic realm for theologians and the intelligible realm for Platonists — and says Pico adds the human person as a fourth [[dougherty2008:004]].

## Stop 3 — Jacob's ladder and the three guides

Pico says the patriarch Jacob teaches through a figure that a ladder stretches from the lowest earth to the highest heavens, marked by a series of many rungs; "God is at its height, and the contemplative angels move up and down it in turns" [[borghesi2012:025]]. Pico says that, inspired by the Cherubic spirit and philosophizing through the grades of nature, we descend and ascend in turn, "until at last, resting in the bosom of the Father Who is at the top of the ladder, we shall be made perfect in theological bliss" [[borghesi2012:028]]. Copenhaver reconstructs the three stages from Pico's text: we should call on Raphael, a heavenly physician, to heal with morals and dialectic; then Gabriel, God's strength, will lead us through Nature; finally he hands us over to the high priest Michael, who bestows the priesthood of theology [[copenhaver2019-angels:086]]. Copenhaver notes that the Oration's seventh exposition of the Cherubic life starts with Zoroaster and its path to happiness ends with angels, who already have the wings that humans need; a healed soul moves through Nature with Gabriel toward Michael's theology, having been healed by Raphael [[copenhaver2019-angels:043]].

## Stop 4 — The Kabbalistic angels in the Oration

Copenhaver observes that angels are everywhere in the Oration, and that Pico's first question to Job — what God wanted from his myriads of angels — is answered by the thesis that God wanted peace among angelic warriors, the Southern Water and the Northern Fire with their Commanders [[copenhaver2019-angels:033]]. The peace on high ends warfare between Michael, commanding the Southern Waters of sefirot 4, and Gabriel, commander of the Northern Fire of sefirot 5, who reconcile at sefirot 6; Gersonides saw their harmony as a fixed order of stars [[copenhaver2019-angels:034]]. Copenhaver adds that Pico took his angel lore in the Job passage from books of Kabbalah, and read Gersonides on Job alongside [[copenhaver2019-angels:036]]. The first of Pico's Kabbalist theses introduces Michael, stationed below the higher angelic choirs but a high priest nonetheless, whose priesthood is that saints be sacrificed by a good death of the kiss; his place is sefirot 4, just beneath the supreme triad [[copenhaver2019-angels:047]].

In the second proem of the *Heptaplus* Pico illustrates the principle of containment by water: the element in our world, the moon in the heavens, and the minds of the Cherubim above the heavens; moisture quenches the heat of life below, feeds it in the heavens, and the supercelestial understands it [[wallis1965-heptaplus:021]]. The water that Pico reads cosmologically in the *Heptaplus* is the same element the Oration reads soteriologically: both accounts arrive at the Cherubim as the figure whose relationship to what is above and below defines the middle term of the whole structure (ed.).

## Stop 5 — The limit at the top of the ladder

Howlett quotes Pico's *Heptaplus* that true and perfect felicity carries us back to union with the beginning, that the angels can be raised to it but cannot ascend to it, and that man cannot go to that level but can be drawn [[howlett2021:168]]. The difference between being raised and being drawn — between a motion imparted from above and a motion powered from within — marks the precise boundary between angelic and human modes of relation to the summit (ed.). Howlett holds that in Pico's ladder the positive attributes of God structure the ascent, while what God is not lies beyond the ladder and beyond the universe [[howlett2021:011]]. Allen suggests that the Oration's vision of man is indebted not only to the Philebus and the Sophist but especially to the Timaeus and Phaedrus myths and to the Timaeus and Parmenides on the universal paradigm and the Idea of Man [[allen2017-other:025]].

The Oration's opening insists that man received a condition to be envied by the intelligences beyond this world precisely because man is not, like them, fixed in a nature that is eternally what it will be [[borghesi2012:003]]. The paradox Pico builds is that the beings who seem to be above man — perfect intellects, unchanging, needing no healing by Raphael — are nevertheless envious; what they lack is the openness that comes from having no fixed seat in the order of things, and which the Oration presents as the condition for becoming not merely angelic but something the angels themselves cannot become on their own power (ed.).
