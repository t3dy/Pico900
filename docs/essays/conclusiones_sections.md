# The *Conclusiones* of 1486: A Section Guide

The *Conclusiones nongentae*, posted in Rome in December 1486 for a public disputation that never took place, are organized in two unequal halves (ed.). The first, *secundum opinionem alienam*, runs to 402 theses drawn from twenty-eight named authorities: six Latin scholastics, eight Arab or Jewish philosophers, five Greek Peripatetics, five Platonists, and four groups of ancient sages ranging from Pythagorean mathematicians to Hebrew Kabbalists (ed.). The second, *secundum opinionem propriam*, runs to 498 theses and is grouped not by authority but by domain: paradoxical propositions, philosophical, theological, Platonic, mathematical, Zoroastrian, magical, Orphic and Kabbalistic (ed.). The Oration was composed as the opening address to the debate; it was not delivered, the Church intervened, and an ecclesiastical commission appointed by Innocent VIII condemned thirteen theses, of which none belonged to the Kabbalistic section, though one of the condemned magic theses invoked Kabbalah [[wirszubski1989-angels:128]].

## The historical sections (T1): *secundum opinionem alienam*

### The Latins (sections 1–6): Albert, Aquinas, Meyronnes, Scotus, Henry of Ghent, Giles of Rome

Six Latin philosophers and theologians open the *Conclusiones*. Thomas Aquinas received by far the most theses of the six — forty-five of the group's 115 — followed by Duns Scotus with twenty-two [[copenhaver2022-angels:050]]. The purpose in all six, as Copenhaver reads it, was not to endorse but to debate: Pico might agree, disagree, or make a third point entirely [[copenhaver2022-angels:050]]. Edelheit draws the implication out further: when Pico appealed to Thomas in his defence of the condemned theses, he found Thomas inconsistent across his own texts, concluding that Thomas for Pico was "not an authority but rather an author and one source among many" [[edelheit2022:090]].

The Aquinas section is the densest of the six and ranges across the core scholastic problems (ed.). Edelheit traces thesis 31, that essence and existence are really distinguished in any created being, directly to Thomas's *De ente et essentia*, where in God alone are they identical while in created intellectual substances existence is limited by the receiving nature [[edelheit2022:072]]. The angels, their location, their illumination of one another, and their relationship to time all receive theses: Edelheit finds thesis 23, that 'one' adds to 'being' only the privation of division, anticipating *De ente et uno*, and locates it in a Thomist quodlibetal question where 'one' adds only a negation of division [[edelheit2022:069]]. Pico's thesis that there is no plurification of angels within one species follows Thomas's *Sentences* commentary (2, d. 3, q. 1, a. 4), where immaterial forms multiply only through matter and two incorporeal angels cannot share a species [[edelheit2022:064]].

Giles of Rome (section 6) appears modestly, with eleven theses, but Edelheit finds him behind some of the more precise angelological propositions (ed.). The thesis that angels were not created in grace is derived by Edelheit from an early Giles commentary, where angels were created with a natural pure love but not in grace [[edelheit2022:105]]. Pico's thesis that a superior angel illuminates an inferior not by presenting a luminous object or dividing what is united but by strengthening the inferior's intellect is found closest in Giles's *Sentences* commentary (2, d. 9, q. 4), where angels mediate between us and God through intellect and illumination [[edelheit2022:109]]. For Albert (section 1), Francis of Meyronnes (3), Henry of Ghent (5), and the smaller scholastic sections, the sources consulted here do not establish which specific theses derive from which specific texts.

Pico argued in the *Apologia* that the scholastic tradition stood at least on equal footing with the patristic and monastic traditions: modern doctors like Ockham disagreed with Pseudo-Dionysius on hierarchical acts, and Scotus "almost destroyed the entire argumentation in Anselm's *Cur deus homo*," which meant, on Pico's view, that modern scholastic reasoning was not subject to the unchallenged authority of the saints [[edelheit2022:095]].

### The Arabs (sections 7–14): Averroes, Avicenna, al-Farabi, and others

The eight Arab and Jewish sections run from Averroes (41 theses, the largest in the group) through Avicenna, al-Farabi, Isaac of Narbonne, Abumaron (Avenzoar), Moses of Egypt (Maimonides), Mohammed of Toledo, and Avempace (2 theses) (ed.). Among these sections the most striking philosophical position is the one the sources consulted here trace to the Averroes section: Still's essay argues that Pico apparently entertained the Averroist doctrine of the unicity of the intellect — one intellect for all human beings, separate from all individuals — against Aquinas and against Ficino [[dougherty2008:014]]. Borghesi's essay reports that Elia del Medigo, who joined Pico in Florence, discussed with him the theories of Avicenna and Averroes on the One, on Being and on Essence, and that Mithridates arrived afterward [[dougherty2008:062]].

Pico regarded Maimonides, listed here as "Moses of Egypt" with three theses, as a Kabbalist rather than a philosopher in the scholastic or Aristotelian mode, and Black says he was particularly influenced by Abulafia's commentary on the *Guide to the Perplexed* [[black2006-a:123]]. What is specific to the remaining Arab sections — al-Farabi, Isaac of Narbonne, Avenzoar, Mohammed of Toledo — is not established by the sources consulted here.

### The Greek Peripatetics (sections 15–19): Theophrastus, Ammonius, Simplicius, Alexander, Themistius

Five sections cover the Greek commentators on Aristotle (ed.). The five together run to only 29 theses; none is large. Simplicius (section 17, 9 theses) appears in *De ente et uno* as an authority on Parmenides: Pico insisted on precision in citing Simplicius, whereas Cittadini was content to follow Simplicius "via Thomas," and Edelheit reads this as exposing two very different scholarly methods — Pico's demand for maximum precision in citation against Cittadini's argument from the conclusion (ed.; see the Ficino-Pico essay for the exchange). Themistius (section 19, 5 theses) comes up in the same exchange: Cittadini pressed that Themistius, if not unknown among the Platonists, shows that the idea which is the foundation of everything is properly being, which would undercut Pico's claim that the Platonists denied God is properly being [[edelheit2022:022]]. What is specific to Theophrastus (4 theses), Ammonius (3), and Alexander of Aphrodisias (8) is not established by the sources consulted here.

### The Platonists (sections 20–24): Plotinus, Adeland, Porphyry, Iamblichus, Proclus

The Platonist group of five contains the largest section in the entire *Conclusiones*: Proclus, with 55 theses, gets more theses than any other single authority in T1 [[copenhaver2019-angels:061]]. His metaphysics of divine triads was, on Copenhaver's reading, crucial for Pico's most ambitious innovation, deriving the Christian doctrine of the Trinity from Kabbalah, even though the Oration names Proclus only once [[copenhaver2019-angels:061]]. Black traces one of the Proclus theses to Proposition 103 of the *Elements of Theology* — all things are in all things, but properly in each — appearing in the Conclusiones as the statement that divine hierarchies are distinct yet everything is in all things in its own mode [[black2006-b:121]].

Ficino read the same Proclean tradition as orthodoxy: on Howlett's account, Ficino held with Plotinian-Proclean orthodoxy that Plato argued the One is above Being [[howlett2021:041]]. Edelheit argues that Pico offered even the opinions of Plotinus and Proclus as further opinions to be examined rather than as received doctrine [[edelheit2022:095]]. Plotinus appears with 15 theses (section 20); Porphyry with 12 (section 22); Iamblichus with 9 (section 23). The section headed "'Adeland the Arab'" (section 21, 8 theses) is the one section whose identification with a historical figure is not established by the sources consulted here.

### The ancient sages (sections 25–28): Pythagorean mathematics, Chaldean theologians, Mercury Trismegistus, Hebrew Cabalists

The final group of historical sections spans Pythagorean mathematical theology (14 theses), the Chaldean theologians (6), Mercury Trismegistus / Hermes (10), and the Hebrew Kabbalists (47) (ed.). Together they carry more theses than several earlier groups and serve a different purpose: where the scholastics and the Greek commentators are being examined as sources, the ancient sages are being read as prisca theologia, secret doctrines that encode the same truths in different symbolic languages [[wirszubski1989-angels:037]].

The Kabbalistic section (28) is the largest of the four and the one for which the sources consulted here have tracked specific sources (ed.). Wirszubski identifies the direct source of the thesis on nine angelic hierarchies (Conclusio II) as Abraham Axelrad's *Corona Nominis Boni*, in a Mithridates Latin translation in Cod. Vat. Ebr. 190, on the ground of the unusual count of nine hierarchies and the unusual spelling Aralim [[wirszubski1989-angels:001]]. Wirszubski further argues that Abraham Abulafia is the source behind Pico's picture of Kabbalah as the divinely revealed explanation of the mysteries of the Law, and that the *De Secretis Legis* (Abulafia's *Sitrey Torah*, in Mithridates's translation) is the only book Pico is known to have read before the *Apologia* that couples the mysteries of the Law with divine names and combination of letters [[wirszubski1989-angels:130]]. Wirszubski notes that some numbers in the Kabbalistic section shift emphasis from the language symbolism of the Hebrew originals to number symbolism in a way Wirszubski says is out of tune with the sources but consistent with the Mithridates translations [[wirszubski1989-angels:118]]. Black says Pico could not have read complete Kabbalistic treatises unaided even in 1489 and approached Kabbalah primarily through the translated corpus and oral contact rather than through original Hebrew texts [[black2006-a:052]].

## The own-opinion sections (T2): *secundum opinionem propriam*

### Sections 1>–4>: paradoxical reconciliations, philosophical, new doctrines, theological

The first four own-opinion sections are thematic rather than organized by thinker (ed.). Section 1> (17 theses) bears the heading *paradoxical reconciliations* and announces the programme of concordance: Pico was engaged in what Borghesi's essay calls "theories on concord," presenting positions whose disagreement he aimed to dissolve rather than adjudicate [[dougherty2008:060]]. Sections 2> (80 theses, against the common philosophical view) and 3> (71 theses, new paradoxical doctrines) develop specific positions, and section 3> contains condemned theses including the claim that it is more improper to say God is intellect than to say the rational soul is an angel [[dougherty2008:026]]. Howlett finds in the section 3> theses on God in the Conclusions an apophatic theology that recalls the Ein-Sof and takes the reader "from Ficinian Platonism to the darkness of Pseudo-Dionysius where blindness and understanding meet" [[howlett2021:115]].

Section 4> (29 theses, theological, against the common mode) contains most of the condemned theses: it is from this section that the question of Christ's descent into hell (4>8), punishment of mortal sin (4>19–20), and adoration of the cross (4>14) were extracted as objectionable (ed.). Pico's appeal to Thomas in the defence of the descent thesis was complicated by the inconsistency Edelheit finds between Thomas's Sentences commentary and the Summa; Pico treated the disagreement as evidence that Thomas was a source to be weighed, not an authority whose verdict settled the case [[edelheit2022:090]]. The same defence showed Pico arguing for the equality of the scholastic with the patristic tradition, with Ockham disagreeing with Dionysius and Scotus demolishing Anselm as precedents [[edelheit2022:095]].

### Section 5>: Plato (62 theses)

Section 5> contains Pico's own Platonic theses (62), the largest of the own-opinion sections after the Kabbalistic (ed.). The sources consulted here do not establish the specific claims of this section beyond what the Ficino essay and the De ente et uno discussion establish about Pico's reading of the Parmenides and Sophist as dialectical exercises and his argument that Plato and Aristotle agree on being and the one (ed.).

### Section 6>: Book of Causes (10 theses)

Section 6> draws on the *Liber de causis*, the Arabic compilation transmitted to the Latin West as Aristotelian before Aquinas identified it from the *Elements of Theology* as Proclean (ed.). The sources consulted here do not establish what specific propositions the ten theses contain beyond this identification of the source tradition.

### Section 7>/7a>: Mathematics (11 + 74 theses)

Section 7>, in two subsections (7> and 7a>), runs to 85 theses on mathematics (ed.). Still's essay notes that the Averroistic unicity thesis is cited in part to theses in 7.2 of the historical section and to 3>67–69 of the own-opinion section [[dougherty2008:014]]. Wirszubski finds that Pico's sense of what constitutes Kabbalistic number is in part shaped by the Mithridates translations, which shift the emphasis from the letter symbolism of the Hebrew originals toward number [[wirszubski1989-angels:118]]. Beyond this the sources consulted here do not establish the specific range of the mathematical theses.

### Section 8>: Zoroaster and Chaldeans (15 theses)

Section 8> is Pico's own theses on the Chaldean oracles, the tradition attributed to Zoroaster and the Persian magi (ed.). Wirszubski examines the thirteenth thesis, which links *puer* to *intellectus*, and judges it likely that the connecting term is Metatron, called *na'ar* (youth) in the Kabbalistic sources and sometimes identified with the Active Intellect, though Wirszubski admits he does not know in what context the interpreters of Zoroaster used *puer* [[wirszubski1989-angels:029]].

### Section 9>: Magic (26 theses)

Section 9> on magic contains 26 theses and one of the most contentious condemned propositions: that no science certifies us of Christ's divinity better than magic and Kabbalah [[wirszubski1989-angels:128]]. The commission's examiner Garsia argued in response that magic and Kabbalah belong to the natural and acquired, not the divine and revealed, and that they cannot certify Christ's divinity more than theology itself — a position that, as Edelheit notes, anticipates the disputes of the 1490s over Savonarola's prophetic authority [[edelheit2022:146]]. Copenhaver reads the magic section as concealing more than it shows: Pico advertised natural magic while keeping quiet about angel magic, since from a prayer addressed to Saint Michael in church it was, as Copenhaver puts it, "a short step to a liturgy for Metatron, a secret but arguably lawful magic invoking a good personal agent with superhuman powers — an angel" [[copenhaver2019-angels:050]].

### Section 10>: Orphic Hymns (31 theses)

Section 10> gives 31 theses on the Orphic Hymns, Pico's reading of the ancient mythological poems as a layer of the same *prisca theologia* encoded in the Chaldean and Kabbalistic traditions (ed.). Wirszubski examines the ninth and tenth Orphic theses: the ninth declares the Curetes of Orpheus identical with the *potestates* of Pseudo-Dionysius, and the tenth requires that whoever attempts the work of the ninth also apply an *opus Cabalae* appropriate to the Fear of Isaac — the Fear of Isaac denoting, in Kabbalistic symbolic language, the fifth sefirah, Might or Judgment [[wirszubski1989-angels:039]]. The two theses thus stitch Orphic theology directly to operative Kabbalah; how the work would be carried out, Wirszubski says, is unknown [[wirszubski1989-angels:039]].

### Section 11>: Kabbalah confirming the Christian religion (72 theses)

The final section, and the longest in the own-opinion half, is the 72 Kabbalistic theses *secundum opinionem propriam*, grouped under the heading that they confirm the Christian religion (ed.). Copenhaver's reading of the section as a whole is that it was intended as the public demonstration of what the Oration called "more secret Hebrew theology": a Kabbalistic confirmation of Christianity that the intended audience of Christian dignitaries could not in fact have grasped, since it relied on Jewish sources and methods entirely outside their formation [[copenhaver2019-angels:066]].

The section opens with ten theses on the angelic hierarchy and the *Merkabot* tradition (ed.). Wirszubski finds the ninth hierarchical thesis's nine-hierarchy count and the form Aralim in Axelrad's *Corona Nominis Boni* [[wirszubski1989-angels:001]]; Copenhaver notes that nine hierarchies, different from Dionysius's nine in naming and order, appear in a Kabbalistic text Pico knew [[copenhaver2019-angels:018]]; Howlett reports that Pico changed the Dionysian nine hierarchies to ten, the number of the sefirot [[howlett2021:094]]. The second group of theses takes up the own-opinion Kabbalistic positions, beginning with the *triplex merchiava* thesis: Wirszubski concludes that the triple Merkabah is a triadic configuration of sefirot, because the fiftieth thesis places the seventh and eighth sefirot in a lower Merkabah [[wirszubski1989-angels:006]].

Wirszubski traces the fourth thesis, that Ensoph is not to be counted among the sefirot but is their abstract and uncommunicated unity, to the Geronese school of Kabbalah and in particular to Rabbi Azriel of Gerona, whose *Quaestiones* reached Pico in Mithridates's translation [[wirszubski1989-angels:053]]. Howlett reads the same thesis as combining Kabbalah with the Plotinian One: Pico embraces Ein-Sof as the unity beyond the sefirot, through a Platonic version of Kabbalism espoused by the Girona School [[howlett2021:080]]. The twentieth thesis, whose symbolism rests on the Hebrew word *'az*, was shaped, on Wirszubski's evidence, by a Trinitarian clause added by the translator with no counterpart in the Hebrew original, connecting the three superior sefirot to the Trinity and forming a specifically Christian reading that the Hebrew text does not support [[wirszubski1989-angels:120]].

The section closes with the theses that directly confirm Christian doctrine from Kabbalistic premises: the Trinity and Incarnation from the sefirot, Metatron as the concordance of ancient theology across traditions, the mechanics of angelic sacrifice [[wirszubski1989-angels:037]]. Copenhaver says that Pico's thesis 11>35 describing God draws on both the Ein-Sof and Christian apophatic theology, taking the reader from Ficinian Platonism toward the darkness of Pseudo-Dionysius [[howlett2021:115]]. None of these Kabbalistic theses was condemned by the commission [[wirszubski1989-angels:128]].
