# The Angelic Material in the *Oration*, the *Commento*, and the *Heptaplus*

This commentary follows the angelic orders through the *Oration*, the *Commento* and the *Heptaplus* (ed.). Pico's *Commento* is not itself in the corpus, so wherever it is reported here the report is a named scholar's, and the scholar is named in the sentence (ed.). In the *Oration* Pico says that by living the life the highest angels live, which man is able to do, we may not be inferior to them and may equal their lot [[borghesi2012:013]]. In the *Commento*, as Michael J. B. Allen reports it, Mind is the first one-and-many, the first angel and the plenitude of the Ideas [[allen2017-venus:041]]. In the *Heptaplus* Pico says the world is the great man, and that Moses represents the intellectual, heavenly and corruptible worlds by the head, the breast and the lower part [[wallis1965-heptaplus:132]]. The claims below are kept in the order
the argument needs, not the order the sources were written (ed.).

## I. The Oration: the angelic orders and the ladder of ascent

Pico opens by setting aside the common arguments for human dignity, among them the argument that
man is only slightly inferior to the angels [[borghesi2012:001]]. He asks instead why the angels
themselves, and the blessed choirs of heaven, should not be admired even more than man
[[borghesi2012:002]]. The universe, on his account, was already complete before man was made: God
had adorned the supercelestial region with intelligences and given the heavenly globes their
souls, and every rank, highest, middle and lowest, was filled [[borghesi2012:004|borghesi2012:005]].
For man, Pico says, if he cultivates his rational seeds he will become a heavenly being, and if he cultivates his intellectual seeds he will be an angel and a son of God [[borghesi2012:007]]. What makes an angel, Pico says, is not separation from the body but spiritual intelligence [[borghesi2012:010]].

Pico's three highest ranks, seraph, cherub and throne, follow a scheme the *Oration*'s editors trace to Pseudo-Dionysius and say is confirmed by Aquinas and Gregory the Great [[borghesi2012:068]]. Pico characterises each in one line: the
seraph burns with the fire of love, the cherub shines with the splendour of intelligence, and the
throne stands in the steadfastness of judgment [[borghesi2012:014]]. The *Oration* then takes up the ranks one at a time (ed.). A man devoted to the active life is strengthened by the steadfast
solidity of the thrones [[borghesi2012:015]]; a man who burns with charity for the Creator alone
will catch fire in the likeness of the seraphim [[borghesi2012:017]]; God flies over the cherub,
who is the contemplator, warming him as if brooding over him [[borghesi2012:018]], a phrase the editors say Pico himself glosses in the *Heptaplus*, where the brooding Spirit of the Lord is the spiritual Olympus, seat of the Spirits of the Lord, warming with its light [[borghesi2012:073]]. The
seraph, who is a lover, is in God and God is in him, so that the two are one
[[borghesi2012:019]]. Crofton Black notes that Pico urges his audience to approach the cherub first, since it provides the exemplar and the path to the others, and that Pico interprets the Dionysian process of being cleansed, illuminated and perfected allegorically as a method of intellectual ascent [[black2006-c:011]]. The cherub, placed in the middle, prepares the soul by his light for the seraphic fire and illuminates for it the judgment of the thrones [[borghesi2012:021]], and the editors gloss the cherub, under the name the order of Pallas, as cherubic contemplation that completes the link between the seraphim's love and the thrones' justice [[borghesi2012:104]]. Pico says that emulating the cherubic life requires first purging the soul through moral science and dialectics, then bathing it in the light of natural philosophy, so that it may be perfected by the knowledge of divine things [[borghesi2012:024]]. Carl Still reads the same three-stage
ladder in the Oration's account of a noonday light that inflames the seraphim and illuminates the
cherubim as evidence that natural philosophy alone cannot complete the ascent, since the highest
illumination needs an infusion from beyond the soul and the order of nature
[[dougherty2008:021]].

Pico says the patriarch Jacob teaches through a figure that a ladder of many rungs stretches from the lowest earth to the highest heavens, with God at its summit and the contemplative angels moving up and down it in turns [[borghesi2012:025]]. The editors note that Pico's depiction of the relatively harmonious comings and goings of the angels on the ladder is a rather joyous contradiction of the typical iconography of the ladder [[borghesi2012:088]]. Whoever would imitate the angelic
life, Pico warns, must approach with clean hands and feet, since it is forbidden by the mysteries
to touch the clean with the unclean [[borghesi2012:026]]. Inspired by the cherubic spirit and philosophising according to the grades of nature, Pico says, we shall descend and ascend in turn until we rest in the bosom of the Father, who is at the top of the ladder, and are made perfect in theological bliss [[borghesi2012:028]]. Pico says the peace God makes in his heavens is the peace the descending angels announced to men of good will, so that men, ascending to heaven, would be transformed by it into angels [[borghesi2012:031]]. He proposes that we call upon Raphael, the heavenly physician, to free us with moral philosophy and dialectics as healing drugs; Gabriel, the strength of God, will lead us through the marvels of nature and deliver us to Michael the high priest, who will bestow the insignia of theological priesthood [[borghesi2012:036|borghesi2012:037]]. The editors gloss this triad as standing in turn for dialectics, natural philosophy and theology [[borghesi2012:107]], and say elsewhere that Pico identifies Michael with the sefirah Keter, which he takes as the symbol of the priesthood [[borghesi2012:103]].

One passage in this material has a disputed text (ed.). Pico writes that the most secret Hebrew theology transforms Enoch into an angel of divinity, named in Hebrew, and at other times reshapes other men into other spirits [[borghesi2012:009]]. The editio princeps leaves a blank where the Hebrew word should stand, and both Basel editions supply a Hebrew retroversion of the Latin words *angelum divinitatis* instead [[borghesi2012:054]]. The editors report that Bausi, following Wirszubski, restores the characters found in the earlier version of the *Oration* in the Florence manuscript Palatino 885 and reads them as Metatron [[borghesi2012:055]]. Wirszubski says he prefers the manuscript reading because Pico's *Commento* warrants its authenticity, and holds that the printed Hebrew phrase may well be a translation of *angelus divinitatis* [[wirszubski1989-angels:036]]. For the blank Hebrew word in thesis T2K10 of the *Conclusions*, Copenhaver holds that Wirszubski's choice of Metatron seems better than Hokmah, Ebgi's preference, because the Agent Intellect, explicitly identified with Metatron by another thesis, better matches the other entities named there [[copenhaver2019-angels:097]]. The printed editions alone therefore leave the word open, and the case for Metatron rests on the manuscript and on the parallel passages (ed.).
The editors point to a parallel passage in the *Commento*, composed in the same year, which states
in almost the Oration's own terms that the Cabalist wise men hold Enoch to have been transformed
into Metatron, angel of divinity, and that this is what is meant more generally by a man's
transformation into an angel [[borghesi2012:056]]. They also quote Gershom Scholem's account that
two distinct traditions were combined in the figure of Metatron, one of an angel created with the
world itself, the other identifying him with Enoch, who ascended to heaven and was changed from a
human being into an angel [[borghesi2012:059]]. Which Enoch text or texts stand behind Pico's
reference remains open: Garin and Tognon point to the Ethiopic Book of Enoch 40.8, but the editors
judge it more likely that Pico drew on a complex and apocryphal textual tradition, possibly
including the Third Book of Enoch, rather than any single source
[[borghesi2012:052|borghesi2012:053]]. The editors say it is perhaps not surprising that the Enoch-Metatron identification occupies a prominent place in Pico's Christian Cabala [[borghesi2012:061]]; elsewhere in the *Oration* Pallas signifies the intellectual nature which leads to wisdom, and in Conclusiones 2.5.14 and 2.11.10 the Orphean Pallas is associated with the cabalists' Metatron [[borghesi2012:105]].

## II. The Commento: the angelic Mind, in Allen's account

Each sentence in this section names the scholar whose account of the *Commento* it gives (ed.). Allen frames Pico,
unlike Ficino, as an Aristotelian by training and an eclectic by conviction rather than a devout
Neoplatonist [[allen2017-venus:001]]. In Allen's account of the *Commento*, Pico prefers a position he attributes to the more perfect Platonists, Plotinus and Porphyry among them: that between the first and third hypostases there is only one Mind, not many minds, which they had even referred to as the son of the father [[allen2017-venus:026]]. This second hypostasis, Mind, is
identified with Saturn and given three functions in Allen's account: a contemplation of what is
above it, later relabelled by Ficino as Uranian regard; a self-contemplation Ficino would later
call self-regarding; and a downward, providential regard for the universe Mind creates
[[allen2017-venus:039]]. Because Mind is the first one-and-many, existing as both the first angel and the plenitude of the Ideas, Allen reports that it is also the first unitary composite, and so the first beautiful thing: not so much the absolute, exemplary Idea of Beauty as the composite, manifold, universal beauty that Ficino would call the splendour of the Ideas [[allen2017-venus:041]]. Elsewhere in the Commento, according to Allen, Pico compares the
Platonists' son of God not to the Son of the theologians but to the first and noblest angel that
God created [[allen2017-other:017]].

In Allen's account, Pico takes Porus, drunk with nectar, to signify the sheer multiplicity of the Ideas, and concludes that although the Ideas of other things existed in the Angelic Mind they were not welcomed to the banquet, because immortality was not granted to them [[allen2017-venus:053]]. Citing Orphic verses, Pico is, in Allen's report, saying that Mind, when first filled with all the Ideas, could rightly be called a
chaos, since the Ideas within it were still imperfect, indistinct and confused
[[allen2017-venus:057]]. Allen traces the resolution of that chaos to an identification of Mind's
unformed substance with the Necessity of Plato's Timaeus: the Ideas first entered Mind under the
reign of Necessity and only became perfected once Mind, in the reign of Love, reverted to God
[[allen2017-venus:066]]. That reversion is what Allen says Pico calls the first circle,
completed when Mind reunites with its own first cause and with the first cause of the Ideas; only
then, on this account, does Venus become an adult, a perfect and sovereign goddess in Mind
[[allen2017-venus:058]]. A parallel passage elsewhere in the Commento, in Carl Still's account,
distinguishes a first love in the Angelic Mind, called angelic or divine, from a second love in the
rational soul, called human love because the rational faculty is the most important part of man's nature [[dougherty2008:017]]. Allen also emphasises the interpretive licence this framework
requires: Pico, he notes, knew that Proclus and the antique interpreters held all traditional myth
to be theological at heart, and that Dionysius the Areopagite had taught, in his own work on the
celestial hierarchy, that the more repellent a myth appears, the more profound its hidden mystery
[[allen2017-venus:062]]. Reading the Commento's model against the Neoplatonic tradition it draws
on, Crofton Black finds little evidence that Platonists in general structured the cosmos the way
Pico proposes there, and observes that even Proclus's own threefold cosmic model in the Elements of
Theology is not consonant with Pico's [[black2006-b:096]]. Allen's own verdict on the whole
construction is double-edged: Pico offers a rich, if not an internally consistent, set of
speculations about the emanative process, and Allen adds that many of the difficulties Pico could
not resolve are themselves intrinsic to Neoplatonism, and to Plotinus's own repeated attempts to
describe the first flowing-out of the One [[allen2017-venus:099]].

In Allen's account, Pico reports that the Cabalist wise men held many of the ancient
patriarchs to have died in a rapture of the intellect, a death the Cabalists call binsica, death
from kissing, and names Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, Moses, Aaron and Mary among them
[[allen2017-venus:011]]. Allen glosses the doctrine directly: through this death the soul not only
separates from the body but the body drops away entirely from the soul, so that we cease to be
humans and become angels, the cherubim and seraphim of Ezekiel's vision
[[allen2017-venus:014]]. Allen also reports Pico's unexpected reading of Orpheus in the third book of the *Commento*: Orpheus was unwilling to die for Eurydice because his music had made him soft and weak, so he descended to Hades for an imaginary Eurydice when he should instead have sent his intellect to gaze on the true Eurydice among the intelligible Ideas [[allen2017-other:075]].

## III. The Heptaplus: the angelic world

In the second proem Pico says he will not imitate those commentators who, in trying to explain the creation of the world, heap
together whatever philosophers and theologians have said anywhere about God, the angels, matter,
the heavens and the whole of nature, and he names Isaac the Persian and Samuel Ophinides as
particular offenders among the Hebrews on this count [[wallis1965-heptaplus:030]]. What follows
instead is an ontology organised around mutual containment: Michael Sudduth's account of the
second proem describes three worlds, a sublunary world inhabited by all living things on earth, a
celestial world of the stars and planets, and an ultramundane, angelic world that theologians
describe as angelic and Platonists as intelligible, to which Pico then adds a fourth world, the
human person [[dougherty2008:004]]. Sudduth argues that this mutual containment is the
metaphysical basis of Pico's whole method of allegorical reading, since discourse about any one
level of reality necessarily draws in the others [[dougherty2008:005]]. Pico illustrates the principle through water: the element in our world, the moon in the heavens, and above the heavens the minds of the cherubim, so that moisture quenches the heat of life below, feeds it in the heavens, and the supercelestial understands it [[wallis1965-heptaplus:021]]. He derives the Hebrew word for the
heavens, which he transliterates asciamaim, from the words for fire and water, so that the
heavens' own composite nature marks their median position between the highest world of light and
the lowest world of darkness [[wallis1965-heptaplus:012]]. Black traces the doctrine's
philosophical genealogy to Proposition 103 of Proclus's Elements of Theology, that all things are
in all things but properly in each, a formula Pico had already used in the section of the
Conclusiones devoted to Proclus before he redeployed it in the Heptaplus
[[black2006-b:121]]. Although Pico credits the doctrine to the Presocratic Anaxagoras, Black finds
that its specific cosmological expression belongs to later Neoplatonism rather than to Anaxagoras
himself [[black2006-b:117]], and elsewhere Black notes an inconsistency in Pico's own scheme: his
stated division of the universe into angelic, celestial and terrestrial worlds would suggest that
the empyrean belongs with the angelic world, yet Pico in fact treats it as merely the foremost of
the ten celestial spheres, the monad that fills out the denary [[black2006-b:086]].

In the Third Exposition Pico says, following Dionysius, that the highest hierarchy has leisure only for contemplation and is symbolised by the waters above the heavens, while the middle rank, assigned to the work of the heavens, is symbolised instead by the firmament itself
[[wallis1965-heptaplus:074]]. Citing Dionysius, Pico says the three angelic activities, purification, illumination and perfection, are distributed so that the lowest order purifies, the middle illuminates and the highest perfects with a fiery and life-giving dew [[wallis1965-heptaplus:081]]. Pico says the Prophet refutes the philosophers who admitted princely intellectual substances but denied that each is set over a multitude of lower rank, and that the leader of each of nine orders is to be imagined as a great sphere with its host as inhabitants [[wallis1965-heptaplus:083]]. Light itself, in Pico's gloss on the first day, was made in the
angel, as the light of intelligible forms, and intelligible species are bound to angelic minds by
an eternal and indivisible bond, unlike the vague and merely customary bond that holds between
such species and the human intellect [[wallis1965-heptaplus:072]]. Black reports that, according to Pico, Dionysius and Thomas Aquinas both hold that angelic intelligence is superior to human because it comprehends with fewer forms; and elsewhere in the Heptaplus Pico says intelligible forms, though accidental to an angel's intellect, are bound to it indivisibly and perpetually, but only loosely to the human intellect [[black2006-b:127]]. Black argues that Moses's text shows how the same concepts can be expressed with very few forms, that is words, and so represents the simplicity of forms by which angels, and by extension prophets, understand things normally out of human reach [[black2006-c:102]]. Sophia Howlett says angels are at least partly imperfect, at one remove from perfection, and quotes Pico's double imperfection in the Heptaplus: that an angel is not being itself but an essence to which being comes by participation, and not intelligence itself but only happens to understand [[howlett2021:166]]. The angelic armies have a further, practical
office in Pico's reading of the waters gathered into one place: following Paul, he identifies them
with the guardian spirits sent to minister to those who receive salvation, assembled to watch over
the good and salvation of man alone [[wallis1965-heptaplus:076]].

Black says the comparison of Dionysius with the Heptaplus shows similarities and differences: Dionysius plucks words from many biblical places in a thematic commentary and concentrates on the ranks of angels and God, whereas Pico follows one chapter of Genesis sequentially and treats the whole cosmic taxonomy below God [[black2006-b:142]]. The figure Pico names
beside Dionysius in this proem, Hierotheus, is identified by the translators as a fictitious mystic referred to as historical by the Pseudo-Dionysius [[wallis1965-heptaplus:144]]. Black thinks it likely that Pico deleted the kabbalistic passages from his final account of esoteric transmission out of prudence, since the bull condemning the Conclusiones, issued by Innocent VIII on 15 December 1487, remained in force until 18 June 1493 [[black2006-b:051]]. The first proem's catalogue of authorities Pico declines to imitate, Black argues, serves a rhetorical purpose: formulated negatively, it lets him display his acquaintance with a wide range of commentaries while implying that he could offer interpretations they had missed [[black2006-a:131|black2006-a:111]].

The Heptaplus is organised as seven expositions: in Black's account of the second proem, one exposition for each of the three worlds, one for man as a fourth world, two on the links between man and the three worlds, and a final exposition corresponding to the sabbath [[black2006-c:072]]. By the second explanation Black gives, everything corresponds to the seven days of creation, and each exposition's seventh chapter turns towards Christ, the end of the law and our sabbath, rest and felicity [[black2006-c:074]]. Black takes the proem to the seventh exposition as a further comment on the structure of the Heptaplus: the first six expositions concern natural felicity, that is philosophy, and the seventh supernatural felicity, that is religion, sacred history from the Fall to redemption [[black2006-c:032]]. Neither division licenses a seven-layer hermeneutic
superimposed on a single verse, and no claim assembled for this commentary supports one (ed.).
Black says the final section of the Heptaplus returns to the word bereshit and abandons allegory for a technique of letter combination derived from kabbalistic exegetical works, producing a sentence that summarises the Heptaplus and confirms Pico's claim that everything can be found in Moses's text [[black2006-a:100]]. Black notes that the
numerological technique of gematria itself, which treats Hebrew letters as numbers, though
pervasive in the kabbalistic corpus that Flavius Mithridates translated for Pico, and particularly
in the works of Abraham Abulafia, is entirely absent from the Heptaplus
[[black2006-b:069]]. Pico's own command of that translated corpus, moreover, has limits Black is
careful to state: on the evidence available one cannot assume Pico could read complete kabbalistic
treatises unaided even by 1489, and his standing as a pioneer among Christian Hebraists rests on
his interest in Kabbalah rather than on unaided linguistic skill, an interest he pursued chiefly
through Mithridates's translated corpus and oral contact [[black2006-a:052]]. Gersonides exerted
what Black calls an undeniable influence on Pico's method of exegesis, visible in Pico's own Job
manuscript and in a Mithridates translation of Gersonides's commentary on the Song of Songs that
Pico owned and annotated, though Black does not think it clear that Pico actually read Gersonides's
commentary on the Pentateuch itself [[black2006-a:126]].

In the Fourth Exposition, Black reports, the Spirit of God moving over the upper waters is identified with a greater and more divine intellect that illuminates the human intellect, and Pico leaves open whether it is God or a mind close and akin to man [[black2006-a:081]]. Black says the tradition of commentary on the two factors of the intellect reached no consensus on whether the active intellect is God, a transcendent entity between God and man, or an aspect of the human soul
[[black2006-c:001]]. In the proem to the Seventh Exposition Pico says that philosophers who spoke only of natural felicity ascribe to the angels no knowledge of God beyond that by which they know themselves, so that they understand God only as his nature is manifest in their own substance [[wallis1965-heptaplus:118]]. Howlett argues that in Pico's universe the angelic mind is the world of forms and that there must be an active intellect in it too [[howlett2021:160]].

## IV. Where Allen and Black converge and diverge, and what remains open

Black's remarks on the *Commento*'s model are the nearest point of contact between Allen and Black in the claims consulted (ed.). Black concedes a connection between Platonism and the threefold model of the *Commento* but finds little evidence that Platonists structured the cosmos as Pico proposes, and notes that Proclus's threefold model is not consonant with Pico's [[black2006-b:096]]. Elsewhere Black describes a shift from the psychological model of the *Commento* to the ontological model of the *Heptaplus*, which he says has other advantages, first among them that it accords with the ontology Aristotle proposes at the beginning of Metaphysics XII, of perishable sensible, eternal sensible and eternal immovable substance, though he judges a direct historical link implausible without later developments in the Aristotelian tradition [[black2006-b:100]]. Read together, Black's remarks describe a shift from a psychological model in the *Commento* to an ontological model in the *Heptaplus* (ed.).

Several questions the claims raise are better left open than resolved by inference (ed.). Whether Pico
consciously overlaid the Kabbalistic sefirot on the Dionysian hierarchy of the Oration, so that the
seraphim correspond to Hesed, the thrones to Din and the cherubim to Binah, is a suggestion the
editors attribute to Farmer, not an established reading of Pico's own text
[[borghesi2012:071]]. Howlett states that Pico changes Pseudo-Dionysius's hierarchy of angels from nine to ten, the number of the sefirot [[howlett2021:094]]. The editors report that the printed editions leave the *Oration*'s Hebrew word blank or supply a back-translation and that Bausi, following Wirszubski, restores Metatron from a manuscript; Copenhaver's own preference for Metatron in T2K10 is hedged [[borghesi2012:054|borghesi2012:055|copenhaver2019-angels:097]]. And the corpus assembled for this commentary returns no supported claim, from
either Allen or Black, on the fallen and demonic angels of the Heptaplus's later expositions, which
is a gap in what the sources so far consulted can establish, not a claim that Pico himself is
silent on the subject (not established by the sources consulted).
