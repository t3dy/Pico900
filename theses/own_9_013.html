Thesis 9>13

Latin

Magicam operari non est aliud quam maritare mundum.

Copy-text: Farmer 1998, line 25280

Copy-text Farmer 1998 (farmer1998:25280), after the Rome print of 7 December 1486 (colophon farmer1998:28040-28042). Akopyan and Mandosio quote the same words (akopyan2018:613; mandosio2012:569).

English

To work magic is nothing else than to wed the world. unverified draft

'magicam' is the accusative of the adjective used as a noun, 'the magical [art]', object of 'operari' (editorial inference from farmer1998:25280). 'maritare' is the vinedresser's verb for training a vine on a tree, the sense of the Oration's image of the farmer who 'marries' elms to vines (busi2014:1567-1568; copenhaver2019:17517-17519).

Translation: machine-drafted 2026-09-25 (agent WRITER-B2), from the Latin, checked against the sense of Farmer's English at farmer1998:25322; awaiting human check

Attribution: Pico's own opinion: the thirteenth magical conclusion, within the series 9>10-14 that Farmer treats as one unit (farmer1998:25280; farmer1998:25295). · Pico's stance: endorses

Doctrine

The thesis defines the whole of magical operation by one act, marriage: the magus joins what is divided in the world (farmer1998:25280). Farmer reads 9>10-14 as one series whose point is that the purified soul of the magician gathers the rational forces scattered through the celestial and terrestrial worlds and raises them; summing up this reading of the series, he writes of 'the views that Pico advances of the magus as cosmic priest' (farmer1998:25295-25297). The same series joins the idea of man as microcosm to the soul's redemption and to the actuation, union and marriage of the world (farmer1998:7290-7293). On this reading the purified magus receives the power to raise fallen nature with himself, 'to marry the world', just as Christ marries the soul prepared by philosophy for the mystical ascent (farmer1998:7284-7287). The Oration spells the image out: as a farmer weds elms to vines, so the magus weds earth to heaven, the lower things to the endowments and powers of the higher (busi2014:1567-1568; copenhaver2019:19650-19652). Magic in this sense does not make its effects but serves nature as she makes them, calling into the light powers sown by God and scattered through the world (copenhaver2019:19637-19640).

Context

Copenhaver traces the axiom to Plotinus, for whom an art of magical love-drawing knits soul to soul as one might train two separate trees towards one another (copenhaver2019:17509-17516). Pico, he writes, turned this into plainer words with help from Roman poets, and then, giving the vineyard image a nuptial turn, took the theme up in this thesis (copenhaver2019:17517-17520). He points to Ovid's Vertumnus, who carried a ladder to climb the elms that bore the ripe fruit of his vineyard, so that the farmer, like the magus, climbed high to bring fruit from above to below (copenhaver2019:17521-17524). Mandosio makes the marriage of the world 'the scope of the magical work', the union of what is above with what is below (mandosio2012:512-514).

Reception

Mandosio argues that for Pico the magus who wishes to perform this marriage needs cabalistic skill to attract the divine emanations and direct their influence to the physical world, which ties the thesis to 9>15 (mandosio2012:512-518). Akopyan reads the image as an alchemical marriage, used by alchemists and made popular by Carl Gustav Jung (akopyan2018:613-614); in his monograph he qualifies the analogy as 'probably' alchemical (akopyan2021:1317-1318). Ebgi uses the same verb for the power of magical characters, which, unlike the signs of conventional languages, can marry the world of things to the world of thought (busi2014:9776-9780; busi2014:10199).

Historiography

Farmer's reading is eschatological: the operative side of Pico's magic belongs to a plan for cosmic salvation fitted to the goals of the Vatican debate (farmer1998:7287-7289). He sets this reading of the magus as cosmic priest against Yates's claim that Pico's magic prepared scientific attitudes to the world (farmer1998:7345-7347). Busi reads the thesis anthropologically: the Piconian magus, like a broker of the cosmos, rejoins what is unpaired and celebrates the nuptials of matter (busi2014:1538-1543). Copenhaver's contribution is philological, identifying the Plotinian axiom and the Roman poets behind the verb (copenhaver2019:17509-17524; copenhaver2019:24166-24167). The disagreement is over what is married: souls and cosmos in a drama of redemption for Farmer, the lower and higher powers of nature in Copenhaver's Plotinian reading, matter paired with matter for Busi (editorial comparison of farmer1998:7284-7287, copenhaver2019:17509-17520 and busi2014:1541-1543).

Pico's sources

  • Oration: 'Et sicut agricola ulmos vitibus, ita magus terram caelo, idest inferiora superiorum dotibus virtutibusque maritat': Pico's own expansion of the image (busi2014:1567-1568)
  • Plotinus, Enneads 4.4.40, on magical love-drawing that knits soul to soul like trees trained towards one another: the philosophical source Copenhaver identifies for the axiom (copenhaver2019:17509-17518)
  • Virgil, Georgics; Horace, Epodes 2.9-10; Ovid, Metamorphoses 14.623-697 (Vertumnus and Pomona): the Roman poets behind the vine-and-elm image (copenhaver2019:24166-24167)

  • Any direct use of the verb 'maritare' in a magical text known to Pico: not established by the sources consulted.
  • A prior draft of this entry quoted Farmer as writing 'homo magus functions as a cosmic priest' at farmer1998:25297. On verification against farmer1998_uploaded.txt this exact clause is not found; Farmer's actual sentence, closing the same discussion of the 9>10-14 series and Yates, reads 'is difficult to reconcile with the views that Pico advances of the magus as cosmic priest' (verified verbatim in this session's uploaded copy, in the 'Summary and conclusions' section immediately following the quotation of 9>13-14). The quotation has been corrected to this verbatim wording; the uploaded copy's own line numbering for this passage does not match the project's registered corpus locator (farmer1998:25297) and the two should be reconciled. A second, independent occurrence of the same phrase ('the magus was not an astrologer but a cosmic priest, intent on redeeming all of fallen nature with himself') appears later in Farmer's book in a discussion of Pico's later Disputationes, confirming 'cosmic priest' as Farmer's own recurring term for this reading, not an invented one.
