Latin

Ens dicitur de deo et creatura uniuoce in quid.

Copy-text: Farmer 1998, line 12240

English

Being is said univocally of God and creature, as belonging to what each is. unverified draft

'Uniuoce': univocally. 'In quid': essential predication.

Translation: original translation, drafted 2026-09-26 by WRITER-swarm-batch5, from Latin at farmer1998:12240

Attribution: Reports Scotus (farmer1998:12240). · Pico's stance: reports

Doctrine

Farmer's note glosses the thesis as reflecting 'the most important Thomist-Scotist conflict that Pico planned to resolve at Rome': Scotus read Thomas as holding that metaphysics deals exclusively with 'individual quiddities', a reading that for Scotus undercut metaphysical knowledge of being qua being, and led him to argue that the being of God and of creatures is univocal rather than merely analogical, as Thomas held (farmer1998:12275). Edelheit confirms that thesis 7 'states that the concept "being" can be attributed in a univocal fashion and according to its essential meaning to both God and a created being', and traces it to Scotus's Lectura 1, distinction 3, part 1, question 1-2, whose second question asks 'whether God is known to us first' (edelheit2022:14132). Farmer adds that Pico's rejection is directed at Scotus's reading of Thomas, not stated here to extend to the univocity claim itself, and that this rejection is suggested elsewhere, in theses 2.42 and 2>5 (farmer1998:12275).

Historiography

Edelheit connects thesis 7 to thesis 8, 'since it comes from the same discussion by Scotus', and states that Pico 'was clearly reading this discussion by Scotus while formulating theses 7 and 8', calling this an instance of the 'concatenatio', an internal connection between different theses (edelheit2022:14228, 14232). He further traces thesis 21 (on substance and its 'proper species') to the same passage of the Lectura, 'in the midst of the same lengthy discussion, "on the cognition of God"' (edelheit2022:15112).
