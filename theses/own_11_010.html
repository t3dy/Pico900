Latin

Illud quod apud Cabalistas dicitur <}N00)9>,,_ illudest sine dubio quod ab Orpheo Pallas, a Zoroastre paterna mens, a Mercurio dei filius, a Pythagora sapientia, a Parmenide sphera intelligibilis, nominatur.

Copy-text: Farmer 1998, line 26593

Farmer's OCR renders the Cabalistic term as an unreadable glyph string; Akopyan prints the Hebrew as ןורטטמ (akopyan2021:3241), and Howlett's translation gives it as 'Metatron' (howlett2021:6261); Busi's edition instead shows a bracketed placeholder, '<lfokmah>' (busi2014:24866), a discrepancy not resolved by the sources consulted.

English

That which is called among the Cabalists [Metatron] is without doubt what is named Pallas by Orpheus, the paternal mind by Zoroaster, the son of God by Mercury (Hermes Trismegistus), wisdom by Pythagoras, and the intelligible sphere by Parmenides. unverified draft

The bracketed term stands for the Cabalistic name Farmer's copy-text does not render legibly (farmer1998:26593); see latin_note.

Translation: original translation, drafted 2026-09-26 by WRITER-swarm-batch1, from Latin at farmer1998:26593

Attribution: Pico's own opinion (farmer1998:26593). · Pico's stance: endorses

Doctrine

The thesis equates a Cabalistic figure with a series of names drawn from the prisci theologi: Pallas in Orpheus, the paternal mind in Zoroaster, the son of God in Hermes (Mercurius), wisdom in Pythagoras, and the intelligible sphere in Parmenides (farmer1998:26593). Farmer's note states that all these symbols correspond to Pico's intellectual or angelic nature (farmer1998:26629).

Context

Farmer cross-references the thesis to 5>4, on Plato, concerning the relation of cause and the perfect and Pherecydes's doctrine that Jove did not fashion the world before being transformed into love (farmer1998:22224).

unverified draft

Historiography

Akopyan quotes the thesis's Latin, printing the Cabalistic term in Hebrew, in a discussion of Pico's Conclusiones Cabalisticae and their later reception (akopyan2021:3241). Busi's edition transcribes the thesis among the conclusions he groups as 'Picus mythicus, magicus, cabalisticus' (busi2014:24866). Wirszubski cites the same Latin text in a discussion of a place in the Conclusiones where "Kabbala and all the illustrious representatives of prisca gentilium theologia appear side by side" (wirszubski1989:10151).
