Thesis 9>20

Latin

Quaelibet uox uirtutem habet in magia, inquantum dei uoce formatur.

Copy-text: Farmer 1998, line 25370

English

Every utterance has power in magic, in so far as it is formed by the voice of God. unverified draft

'uox' spans 'spoken sound', 'utterance' and 'word'; the thesis follows directly from 9>19's claim that words have magical efficacy because the first exercise of magic by nature is the voice of God (farmer1998:25366). Farmer's note ties the doctrine to word-magic and to God's creation of the world through speech in Genesis 1, mediated, on the Kabbalists' account, by the divine names and the Hebrew alphabet (farmer1998:25400).

Doctrine

The thesis draws a conclusion from the immediately preceding one: because the first exercise of magic by nature is the voice of God, any utterance has magical power in so far as it is formed by that voice (farmer1998:25366; farmer1998:25370). Farmer's note connects the doctrine to word-magic generally and to God's creation of the world through speech in Genesis 1:1ff., mediated, according to the kabbalists, by the powers of the divine names and the Hebrew alphabet (farmer1998:25400). Akopyan's and Mandosio's citations show the thesis sitting within a short run on the sources of magical efficacy, alongside 9>21 on non-significative versus significative sounds and 9>24 on the power of characters and figures (busi2014:1616; mandosio2012:477).

Context

Mandosio quotes the thesis together with 9>19 as evidence that Pico grounds the vocal component of magic in the divine origin of speech, immediately before a further thesis restricting magically effective names to Hebrew ones or those directly derived from it (mandosio2012:477). Busi and Ebgi's edition numbers it as Conclusiones magicae 9>20, between 9>19 (unnumbered continuation) and 9>21 on non-significative sounds (busi2014:1616; busi2014:1617).

unverified draft

Historiography

Akopyan and Mandosio both cite the thesis to illustrate Pico's theory of the natural, divinely grounded efficacy of magical language, in each case quoting it as part of a longer run of theses rather than commenting on it individually (akopyan2018:626; akopyan2021:1351; mandosio2012:477). Busi and Ebgi's apparatus records its place in Pico's own numbering, distinguishing it from the adjacent theses 9>21 and 9>24 (busi2014:1617; busi2014:26629).
