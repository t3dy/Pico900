Latin

Cum dixit Iob, qui facit pacem in excelsis suis, aquam intellexit australem et ignem septentrionalem, et praefectos illorum de quibus non est ultra dicendum.

Copy-text: Farmer 1998, line 17969

Apparatus (Farmer): 1486 Cum dixit.lob.qui

Copy-text Farmer 1998 (farmer1998:17969-17971), u/v as Farmer prints them. The 1486 print punctuates 'Cum dixit.Iob.qui' (farmer1998:17981). Wirszubski inserts the reference Job 25:2 and a comma after 'illorum' (wirszubski1989:2313-2315).

English

In saying 'who makes peace in his high places', Job had in view the water of the south and the fire of the north, together with those placed in command of them, of whom no more may be said. unverified draft

'qui facit pacem in excelsis suis' is the Vulgate of Job 25:2 (farmer1998:18050). 'praefectos' is rendered 'those placed in command'; Farmer, following a Bahir passage, identifies them as the archangels Michael and Gabriel (farmer1998:18051-18053). 'non est ultra dicendum' is a gerundive of obligation, 'no more is to be said', and can carry a prohibition as well as a reticence (editorial note on farmer1998:17970-17971).

Doctrine

The thesis glosses Job 25:2, 'he makes peace in his high places' (farmer1998:18050). Wirszubski notes that this interpretation of the verse is traditional, incorporated in the Aramaic version of Job and in Rashi's commentary (wirszubski1989:2317-2318). The Bahir passage that he prints explains the verse by the two archangels: Michael stands at God's right, with water and hail, Gabriel at his left, with fire, and God makes peace between them (wirszubski1989:2354-2360). Farmer's note to the neighbouring thesis 28.6 records that 'north' or the 'north wind' was a regular kabbalistic symbol of the fifth sefirah (farmer1998:10279); this uploaded copy of Farmer 1998 does not carry a note specifically identifying the sefirot behind 28.24's 'southern water' and 'northern fire', or elaborating any further association of the fifth sefirah with Satan or the origins of evil (see not_established). Recanati's system, as Corazzol reconstructs it, gives the fourth sefirah the property of piety and the fifth that of judgment, which brings calamity when it acts alone (corazzol2019:474-480; not independently checked in this session, as no corazzol2019 file was among the uploaded sources consulted). Read with the Bahir, the thesis makes the peace of Job 25:2 a balance between two opposed divine properties and their angelic governors (editorial inference from wirszubski1989:2354-2360). The closing clause withholds the names of the governors, and the Bahir supplies them (editorial inference from wirszubski1989:2357-2359).

Context

Wirszubski found that Pico could have read the Bahir passage in three of his manuscripts: the Chigi manuscript that once contained the whole of Ma'areket ha-Elohut, the Vatican Hebrew manuscript 191 that includes the Bahir, and the lost translation of Recanati, who quoted the passage (wirszubski1989:2318, 2344-2348). Because the first and third translations are lost, he refrains from naming the direct source and quotes the Vatican text only by way of illustration (wirszubski1989:2349-2351). The larger dispute over Pico's dependence on Flavius Mithridates' translations bears on this thesis: Farmer argues that Mithridates' version of Recanati was completed only after the theses were printed (farmer1998:17441-17451). The verse returns in Pico's Oration, where Job, asked what God most desires among the myriads that attend him, answers peace, 'Qui facit pacem in excelsis' (borghesi2012:1593-1596). There Empedocles, the philosopher, interprets the words of Job, the theologian, through the twin natures in the soul, strife and friendship, war and peace (borghesi2012:1597-1601). Edelheit, discussing the same passage of the Oration, writes that Pico's statement that 'the philosopher Empedocles should interpret for us the words of the theologian Job' is justified by the hermeneutical principle that 'the middle order should interpret the precepts of the supreme order for those in the lower order' (edelheit2022:4892-4896).

Reception

Borghesi, Papio and Riva, commenting on the Oration's use of Job 25:2, cross-refer the reader to this thesis (borghesi2012:1656-1657). Pico's own second Cabalistic set turns the same pair of symbols to Christian ends, claiming that the Cabala shows 'most openly' why the Son of God came with the water of baptism and the Holy Spirit with fire (farmer1998:27335-27336). Farmer links the two theses and points to 28.1 and 11>11 for the mystical role of Michael, the priest who sacrifices rational souls (farmer1998:18050-18055, 17532-17533). No response to 28.24 by the papal commission or by Pico's contemporaries is recorded in the sources consulted (see not_established; farmer1998:17969).

Historiography

Wirszubski's discussion establishes three things: the exegesis is traditional, the Bahir supplies the angels, and the exact channel to Pico cannot be fixed (wirszubski1989:2317-2318, 2349-2351); this uploaded copy of Farmer 1998 does not carry, at this thesis, any note tying the angelic identification to a specific pair of sefirot (see not_established, and farmer1998:10279 for the one sefirah-identification Farmer's notes do confirm nearby, for thesis 28.6). The Oration's pairing of Job and Empedocles, read beside Pico's own thesis 3>71 that proportions Empedocles' strife and friendship to two sefirot (wirszubski1989:9641-9645), shows Pico treating the peace of Job 25:2 as a point where Hebrew theology and Greek natural philosophy say the same thing (editorial inference from borghesi2012:1597-1601 and edelheit2022:4892-4896).
