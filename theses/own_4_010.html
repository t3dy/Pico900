Latin

Illa uerba (hoc est corpus, etc.), quae in consecratione dicuntur, materialiter tenentur non significatiuae.

Copy-text: Farmer 1998, line 21666

Copy-text Farmer 1998, OCR, which prints 'significatiuae' (farmer1998:21666-21667). Copenhaver gives the Conclusions' wording as 'et cetera' and emends 'significatiuae' to 'significatiue' (copenhaver2022:9476-9478). He notes that the Apology's statement of the thesis differs slightly from the Conclusions (copenhaver2022:9463-9466). Garcia's 1489 title, as printed by Edelheit, reads 'hoc est corpus meum' and 'materialiter tenentur et non significative' (edelheit2022:20775-20777).

English

The words spoken in the consecration ('hoc est corpus' and the rest) are taken materially, not significatively. unverified draft

'materialiter' and 'significatiue' are terms of supposition theory: a term in material supposition stands for the word itself, as 'frog' does in 'frog has four letters', while in personal or significative supposition it stands for the things the word signifies (copenhaver2022:983-986, 9696-9700). 'tenentur' is rendered 'are taken', the logicians' verb for how a term is used in a proposition, the verb Copenhaver also uses (copenhaver2022:9469-9470). The parenthesis abbreviates the formula 'hoc est enim corpus meum', this is my body (copenhaver2022:966-969).

Condemnation

Q10 in the order of the Apology: Eucharist: words of consecration. Printed 7 December 1486; censured by the papal commission in February-March 1487; the work as a whole condemned by the bull of 4 August 1487.

Doctrine

The thesis answers the puzzle of 'hoc': when the priest says 'this', what does the demonstrative pick out (copenhaver2022:968-971)? It cannot be the bread, which is not Christ's body, nor Christ's body, which is not present until the sentence that begins with 'hoc' has been finished (copenhaver2022:970-973). Supposition theory distinguished what a term signifies in isolation from what it stands for, or supposits for, within a proposition (copenhaver2022:987-993). A term in material supposition stands for a word, a term in personal or significative supposition for the things the word signifies (copenhaver2022:9696-9700). To take the words materially, in Farmer's gloss, is to put them in quotation marks; taken otherwise, 'this is my body' in a priest's mouth would refer to the priest's own body (farmer1998:21745-21750). Pico's defence restricts the thesis to the priest: the words were significative when Christ spoke them and are material when a priest narrates (recitat) them, so that the priest's phrase supposits for Christ's significative phrase (copenhaver2022:10066-10072). His evidence is the rest of the rite: the priest says 'take and eat' though he addresses no apostles, and 'which for you and for many is shed' though no blood is shed at that moment, so these words are true only as narration (copenhaver2022:10076-10092). Thesis 2>16 denies that the Treatise on Supposition belongs to logic; Farmer comments that Pico used the language of supposition theory only occasionally, as here, and regarded departures from traditional Aristotelian logic with disdain (farmer1998:19122-19126).

Context

Pico's thesis condenses the Ordinary Gloss on canon 25 of De consecratione, which says that 'hoc' indicates nothing because it is used materially, and that a priest who said the words significatively would be lying (copenhaver2022:9535-9551). The narrative reading had a long history before Pico (copenhaver2022:9842-9845). Lotario dei Segni, later Innocent III, held that the priest uses the words not declaratively but narratively (copenhaver2022:9615-9643). Aquinas rejected the view that the whole utterance is taken materially when spoken as narrative, because the words would then not apply to a present body and the sacrament could not be carried out (copenhaver2022:9738-9747). Scotus held the formula true only at the last instant of its utterance and allowed that the priest's words are narrated (copenhaver2022:9755-9765). Among the theses Pico reported from Scotus, 4.15 states that consecration cannot be made by the bare words 'Hoc est corpus meum' without the narrative words that precede them (farmer1998:12357-12358). Durand de Saint-Pourçain argued that the priest speaks 'recitative et non significative' (copenhaver2022:9766-9772, 9799). Ockham treated the problem through his distinction between spoken and mental language, and Wyclif agreed with Thomas that the glossator's account made the sacrament ineffective (copenhaver2022:9803-9810). Jean Cabrol, defending Thomas, gave 'hoc' a merely confused supposition (copenhaver2022:9811-9825). Copenhaver concludes that Pico relied on terms of art, materialiter, significative and recitative, that had been developing since the twelfth century (copenhaver2022:9842-9845).

Reception

The commission judged the thesis scandalous and contrary to the usual view of saintly teachers, and added that the glossator's view on the point does not hold (copenhaver2022:8902-8903, 10060-10062). Farmer reads the condemnation of an apparently innocuous thesis as a sign of the commission's hostility to Pico (farmer1998:21750-21752). Copenhaver agrees that Pico had logic and law on his side, and explains the verdict by the company the thesis kept: the judges read it beside Q6 and Q9, where the doctrinal stakes were higher and the insults to the court provocative (copenhaver2022:1015, 9551-9555). Pico turned the charge around, calling the commission's finding the scandalous one and his own view not only Catholic but more acceptable (probabilior) than its opposite (copenhaver2022:10062-10065, 9906-9909). He reminded the judges that the Gloss had never been rejected by the Church (copenhaver2022:9905-9907). Pedro Garcia (Garsia), a judge who published a reply in 1489, gave a philosophical account of 'hoc' in which the pronoun indicates determinately neither the terminus to which nor the terminus from which of transubstantiation (copenhaver2022:548-549; edelheit2022:20756-20758, 20775-20782). Giovanni Caroli answered the thesis together with the other two eucharistic theses (edelheit2022:21280-21283, 21711-21714).

Historiography

Farmer presents the thesis as an instance of Pico's use of late medieval supposition theory and calls it apparently innocuous (farmer1998:21745-21751). Copenhaver reconstructs the canon-law and scholastic background and reads the thesis as a précis of the Gloss that would have seemed harmless in other circumstances (copenhaver2022:9551-9553). He observes that the thesis itself says nothing about narration, which enters only with the defence, and that Pico's 'supponit' could be read in the ordinary sense of 'substitutes for', as an actor stands in for a character (copenhaver2022:9870-9878). He also judges the Apology's Q10 too short to tell whether its text needs emending (copenhaver2022:8958-8959). Edelheit groups the thesis with 4>1 and 4>2 as the eucharistic theses that Caroli attacked, and describes Garcia's account of 'hoc' as evidence that scholastic philosophy was worked out inside theological questions (edelheit2022:20751-20758, 21699-21714). The case shows a thesis that repeated standing canon law being censured for its setting, which is the reading Copenhaver gives (copenhaver2022:9551-9555, 9905-9907).
