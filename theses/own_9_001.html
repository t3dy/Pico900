English

The whole of the magic practised by the moderns, which the Church rightly roots out, lacks all stability, all foundation and all truth, since it hangs on the hand of the foes of the first truth, these powers of darkness who spread the shadows of falsehood over ill-disposed minds. unverified draft

'pendet ex manu' is literally 'hangs from the hand', i.e. depends on the agency of (farmer1998:25113). 'potestatum harum tenebrarum' echoes Col. 1:13, as Farmer notes (farmer1998:25172). 'exterminat' is rendered 'roots out' in the sense of 'drives out of its bounds'; Busi's Italian has 'sradica' (busi2014:1493-1494). 'prima ueritas' is God as first truth, the enemies being demons (editorial inference from farmer1998:25113-25114 and the cross-reference to 22.9 at farmer1998:25172).

Doctrine

The thesis opens the series by setting aside one kind of magic before any positive claim is made: the magic current among the moderns is condemned, and condemned on grounds of truth, since it has no firmness, no foundation and no truth (farmer1998:25111-25113). The reason given is causal: such magic depends on the enemies of the first truth, the 'powers of darkness' of Col. 1:13, which cloud minds already badly disposed (farmer1998:25113-25114; farmer1998:25172). Farmer cross-refers the demonology to 22.9, where Porphyry divides evil demons into substantial souls and material, accidental powers (farmer1998:25172; farmer1998:15550). The thesis thus defines by exclusion the term 'goetia' used in the Oration: the magic that relies wholly on the work and authority of demons, which the Greeks called goeteia and never dignified with the name of magic (copenhaver2019:19582-19588). In the Oration Pico calls that kind the most dishonest of arts, hollow and useless, against a magic that is solid, strong and reliable (copenhaver2019:19595-19596). The three denials of 9>1 (no firmness, no foundation, no truth) answer point for point to that contrast (editorial inference from farmer1998:25112 and copenhaver2019:19595-19596).

Context

Copenhaver shows how little the late-antique legacy offered Pico for a clean line between good and bad magic: Augustine's was the usual Christian position, that magic needs demons, with whom any communication is evil (copenhaver2019:17349-17354). Copenhaver observes that the first four magical propositions distinguish good magic from bad in the same terms used by the Oration (copenhaver2019:17364-17365). Akopyan likewise reads 9>1-2 as a repetition in the Conclusions of the Oration's two forms of magic (akopyan2018:543-552). Busi treats the disclaimer as more a confession than an excuse, since the reputation of Pico the magician outlived him and Benivieni still had to deny it in a letter of 1514 (busi2014:1496-1500; busi2014:1558-1559).

Reception

In the Apology Pico invoked this thesis when defending a later conclusion, writing of his magical conclusions that his judges had no cause to suspect them, 'cum sicut dixi in prima conclusione, refellam omnem magiam prohibitam ab Ecclesia illam damnans et detestans protestans me solum loqui de magia naturali' ('since, as I said in the first conclusion, I reject all magic prohibited by the Church, condemning and denouncing it, protesting that I speak only of natural magic'), quoting the Apology (Opera, 166-168) at second hand through Edelheit's citation (edelheit2022:5617). Busi and Ebgi summarise the Apology's argument as treating magic as an equivocal term, one sense rightly banished by the Church (busi2014:9849-9856). Farmer judges that the magical theses, despite all disclaimers, were always open to suspicions of demonology and witchcraft (farmer1998:1355-1357). Voltaire read the first proposition against its intent: from its words, he wrote, one sees that Pico would grant that magic is the work of demons, as commonly believed (copenhaver2019:8916-8920). Copenhaver cites this passage of the Essai among the jibes by which Voltaire reduced Pico to a figure of fun (copenhaver2019:8951-8952; copenhaver2019:23036).

Historiography

Copenhaver reads the first four theses as a public strategy, advertising natural magic while keeping quiet about angel-magic (copenhaver2019:17361-17365). Busi reads it as a confession of the suspicion Pico expected, and quotes the thesis as the beginning of the first of the twenty-six magical conclusions (busi2014:1496; busi2014:1514-1516). The thesis matters for the whole series because the Apology later made it the explicit limit on every magical conclusion that followed (busi2014:9914-9917).
