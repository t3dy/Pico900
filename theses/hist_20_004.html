Latin

Anima quae peccauit uel in terreno uel in aereo corpore post mortem bruti uitam uiuit.

Copy-text: Farmer 1998, line 15094

English

The soul that has sinned, whether in an earthly or an aerial body, lives the life of a brute after death. unverified draft

The thesis states a doctrine of metempsychosis into non-rational animal life as punishment for sin, attributed to Plotinus; 'aereo corpore' reflects the Neoplatonic notion of an intermediate, aerial vehicle of the soul distinct from its earthly body (farmer1998:15094).

Translation: original translation, drafted 2026-09-26 by WRITER-full-batch8, from Latin at farmer1998:15094

Attribution: Reports Plotinus (farmer1998:15094). · Pico's stance: reports

Doctrine

The thesis holds that a soul which has sinned, whether in an earthly or an aerial body, lives the life of a brute animal after death (farmer1998:15094). Farmer's note cross-references three other theses on the same topic of the transmigration of souls: 21.8, attributed to 'Adeland the Arab,' which states that all the wise men of the Indians, Persians, Egyptians, and Chaldeans believed in the transcorporation of souls (farmer1998:15366); Pico's own thesis 5>51, which Farmer glosses as opposing Plotinus, arguing from a saying of Plato in the Phaedrus that, rightly understood, it shows Plotinus's position positing the transmigration of souls into brutes to be mistaken (farmer1998:23083); and 8>4, on transanimation into plant life, which Farmer connects to a similar view in Empedocles (farmer1998:24832). Farmer's note also cites Plotinus's Enneads 1.1.11 and following as the classical source (farmer1998:15126).
