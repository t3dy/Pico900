Latin

Frustra adit naturam et protheum, qui pana non attraxerit.

Copy-text: Farmer 1998, line 26127

English

In vain does he approach nature and Proteus who has not drawn Pan to himself. unverified draft

'pana' and 'protheum' are Pico's oblique-case Latinizations of the Greek names Pan and Proteus, kept as proper names (farmer1998:26127).

Translation: original translation, drafted 2026-09-26 by WRITER-full-batch18, from Latin at farmer1998:26127

Attribution: Pico's own opinion. · Pico's stance: endorses

Doctrine

The thesis holds that approaching 'nature and Proteus' is futile without first having drawn 'Pan' to oneself (farmer1998:26127). Farmer's note glosses Pan (Greek for 'All') as the intellectual nature named as the 'first form' in the preceding-but-one thesis, glosses Proteus tentatively as standing for man, and cross-references Pico's Oration and a study by Garin (farmer1998:26158).

unverified draft

Context

Farmer's note further records that Athanasius Kircher, in his Oedipus Aegyptiacus, later interpreted Pan in this thesis as the 'one' uniting the 'many' (farmer1998:26158). The thesis belongs to the Orphic Hymns own-opinion series (farmer1998:25560), and Farmer cross-references it to thesis 10>26, on returning perfectly into the soul (farmer1998:26118), to thesis 5>52, on Socrates's prayer to Pan (farmer1998:23089), and to thesis 4>8, among the theological conclusions, on Christ's descent to hell (farmer1998:21657).

unverified draft

Reception

Busi and Ebgi print the thesis's Latin text verbatim and cross-reference it to the eleventh Orphic Hymn (busi2014:24979; busi2014:19679).

Historiography

Busi and Ebgi, and Farmer's own note, are the only discussions of this thesis in the sources consulted; no dispute over its interpretation is recorded there (busi2014:24979; farmer1998:26158).
