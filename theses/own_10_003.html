Thesis 10>3

Latin

Nomina deorum quos Orpheus canit non decipientium demonum, a quibus malum et non bonum prouenit. Sed naturalium uirtutum diuinarumque sunt nomina, a uero deo in utilitatem maxime hominis, si eis uti sciuerit, mundo distributarum.

Copy-text: Farmer 1998, line 25580

English

The names of the gods that Orpheus sings are not the names of deceiving demons, from whom evil and not good proceeds. Rather, they are the names of natural and divine powers, distributed through the world by the true God chiefly for man's benefit, if he knows how to use them. unverified draft

Farmer's edition marks this thesis as the 800th in his cumulative numbering of the whole collection (farmer1998:25636).

Doctrine

Farmer reads the thesis as making the Orphic gods symbols of whatever material or spiritual 'powers' lay hidden in the sublunary, celestial, and (probably) angelic worlds (farmer1998:25657).

unverified draft

Context

The thesis follows Pico's defense, at 9>1-2, of natural magic as licit and not prohibited, and extends that defense to the Orphic gods by denying they are demonic (farmer1998:25111; farmer1998:25117).

Historiography

Busi and Ebgi quote the thesis's Latin from their edition, Conclusiones, page 504 (their note on thesis 10>3), in a form with minor orthographic variants from Farmer's copy-text ('provenit' for 'prouenit', 'divinarumque' for 'diuinarumque') (busi2014:17278).
