Latin

Eaedem sunt litterae nominis cacodemonis qui est princeps mundi huius et nominis dei Triagrammaton, et qui sciuerit ordinare transpositionem deducet unum ex alio.

Copy-text: Farmer 1998, line 17862

English

The letters of the name of the cacodemon, who is the prince of this world, are the same as those of the name of God Triagrammaton, and whoever knows how to arrange the transposition will derive the one name from the other. unverified draft

'cacodemon' (Greek kakodaimon, 'evil spirit') here names Satan, called 'prince of this world' (wirszubski1989:2122). 'Triagrammaton' is a name for God whose letters Farmer's note, citing Wirszubski, describes as transposable with those of 'cacodemon' by gematria (farmer1998:17937). The doctrine rests on a specific wordplay traceable, per Wirszubski, to a Hebrew commentary in Flavius Mithridates' Latin translation (wirszubski1989:3276).

Doctrine

The thesis claims that the name of the cacodemon who rules this world shares its letters with the divine name Triagrammaton, so that whoever knows the correct transposition can derive either name from the other (wirszubski1989:2081, quoting the same Latin text as Farmer's copy). Farmer's note explains that the transformation is a matter of word-letter equations (gematria), citing Wirszubski's quotation of a passage from Mithridates' translation of a commentary on the Book of Creation (farmer1998:17937). Wirszubski, quoting the Latin translation itself, gives it as 'nomen ipsius dei triagramaton [...] continet in secreto cacodemona qui est princeps mundi huius', which he says 'sufficiently accounts for Pico's thesis', while wondering whether Pico himself knew precisely how to carry out the letter-derivation (wirszubski1989:2122).

Context

Wirszubski calls the underlying commentary 'the direct source of Conclusio xix' and adds that it is 'remarkable that [this is] the only thesis in the entire first set which deals with names and transpositions of letters without any…' (the excerpt available breaks off there) (wirszubski1989:3276; wirszubski1989:3277). Farmer's note relates the thesis to the broader kabbalistic principle of parallel 'right-hand' and 'left-hand' orders (farmer1998:17937).

Historiography

Wirszubski is the only scholar in the sources consulted who works out the textual derivation of the thesis, tracing it to Flavius Mithridates' Latin rendering of a commentary on the Book of Creation and judging, from the translation's wording, that it 'sufficiently accounts for Pico's thesis' even without certainty that Pico could reconstruct the letter transposition himself (wirszubski1989:2122; wirszubski1989:3276).
