Latin

Illius natura quae est orizon aeternitatis temporalis est mago proxima, sed super eum, et ei propria est cabala.

Copy-text: Farmer 1998, line 25362

Copy-text Farmer 1998 (farmer1998:25362-25363), after the Rome print of 7 December 1486 (colophon farmer1998:28040-28042); Farmer's OCR 'temporalisest' is read 'temporalis est'. Farmer records no variant for this thesis (farmer1998:25385-25387). Wirszubski, quoting the Opera, prints 'ei proxima est Cabala' for 'ei propria est cabala' (wirszubski1989:9918-9921; wirszubski1989:9939). Mandosio proposes emending 'Illius natura' to 'Illa natura', as in 9>16 (mandosio2012:561-562); the edition keeps the printed reading and translates it.

English

The nature of what forms the horizon of temporal eternity stands nearest the magus, yet above him, and Kabbalah belongs properly to it. unverified draft

'orizon' (horizon): the boundary-line of an order of being; the three horizons of 9>16-18 are glossed together in Farmer's note (farmer1998:25393-25395). 'ei' is taken to refer to the nature, on the model of 'Illius naturae ... propria est magia' in 9>17 (farmer1998:25358); Copenhaver's 'Kabbalah belongs to him' (copenhaver2019:20307-20308) allows it to be read of the magus. With Mandosio's emendation (mandosio2012:562) the subject would be 'that nature which is the horizon'.

Doctrine

The thesis is the third of three on 'horizons': 9>16 places a nature next to the magus but below him, 9>17 makes magic proper to the nature of the horizon of time and eternity, and 9>18 places a nature next to the magus but above him and gives it Kabbalah (farmer1998:25354-25363). Wirszubski took the three phrases to denote one and the same thing, the dividing line between the dimension of time and the dimension of eternity (wirszubski1989:9923-9926). Farmer judges that this view 'is clearly mistaken' and reads the three horizons as references, in order, to the corporeal, rational and intellectual natures (farmer1998:25393-25396). On his reading magic belongs with the rational part of the soul, the nature proper to man, as in 9>14, and 'pure Cabala' with the intellectual part, as in 11>12 (farmer1998:25396-25398). Farmer also sets the series within the expansion of aevum, the intermediate duration between time and eternity, into a hierarchy of durational states (farmer1998:11569-11573). Aevum itself arose, he explains, from reconciling texts that gave eternity to God alone with texts that placed angels or demons in it, by distinguishing eternity per se from a participated eternity, the duration of immutable but created beings (farmer1998:3691-3695). Mandosio, who also rejects Wirszubski's reading, maps the horizons onto disciplines: eternal time is inferior to magic and corresponds to natural philosophy, the horizon of time and eternity corresponds to magic, and temporal eternity, superior to magic, corresponds to cabala (mandosio2012:505-509; mandosio2012:559-561). The magician, he concludes, has an intermediary status between the natural philosopher and the cabalist (mandosio2012:509-511).

Context

Farmer cites 9>16-18 and 11>12 as the evidence that one kind of Cabala, for Pico, invoked intellectual or angelic powers, while the Apology discusses at length another part 'that concerns the powers of celestial bodies' (farmer1998:7015-7017; farmer1998:7041). He concludes that Pico recognised hierarchical distinctions of power within both natural magic and Cabala, as his syncretic system would lead one to expect (farmer1998:7109-7114). Copenhaver draws the same boundary in cosmic terms: nature's magical powers rise only to the horizon between time and eternity, and only Kabbalah can empower magic to cross it (copenhaver2019:17385-17389). A magic with supernatural ambitions, he adds, might address the Sefirot without ascending to them, which only Kabbalah could do (copenhaver2019:17389-17391).

Reception

Mandosio finds an echo of Pico's horizons in the twenty-third theorem of John Dee's Monas hieroglyphica, whose diagram sets a 'horizon of time' at the limit of the celestial and supercelestial worlds and a 'horizon of eternity' at the upper limit of the supercelestial (mandosio2012:520-529). He attributes the first observation of the echo to Clulee (mandosio2012:570). Howlett quotes the thesis to argue that Pico's Kabbalism is not the magic of the theurgic magus found in Ficino but a different, higher form of magic than the gnostic and Platonic one, 'more connected to the Pythagorean use of math' (howlett2021:5502-5511).

Historiography

Three keys to the series have been proposed: Wirszubski's single boundary between time and eternity (wirszubski1989:9923-9926), Farmer's ladder of corporeal, rational and intellectual natures (farmer1998:25393-25396), and Mandosio's ladder of natural philosophy, magic and cabala (mandosio2012:505-509). Wirszubski counts this thesis among three magical conclusions that each assert the superiority of Kabbalah over magic (wirszubski1989:9908-9911). Ebgi quotes it as the consequence of a prior ranking, the superiority of numbers over figures, to which a superiority of Kabbalah over magic corresponds (busi2014:10163-10186; busi2014:10199). His note concedes that the relation of magic and Kabbalah in Pico is complex and little studied, since for Pico Kabbalah is said in many ways, one of which seems to coincide with natural magic (busi2014:10207-10210). Howlett suggests that the horizon of temporal eternity could be the intellectual world, giving Pico a direct route into it, and cites Farmer's note (howlett2021:5507-5509; howlett2021:6205). The thesis ranks Kabbalah above magic by the height of the nature it belongs to, not by a list of operations, which is why each reader's account of the three horizons settles what Kabbalah is here (editorial inference from farmer1998:25362-25363 and farmer1998:25393-25396).
