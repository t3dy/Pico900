Latin

Obiectum per se et proprie sensus communis est magnitudo, ut bene dixit Auicenna.

Copy-text: Farmer 1998, line 11053; 1486 print folio <1r/1v>

English

The object of the common sense, in itself and properly, is magnitude, as Avicenna well said. unverified draft

'sensus communis' is Farmer's 'special faculty' for perceiving objects common to more than one external sense, not the modern colloquial sense of the phrase (farmer1998:11125).

Translation: original translation, drafted 2026-09-26 by WRITER-swarm-batch7, from Latin at farmer1998:11053

Attribution: Reports Albert the Great (farmer1998:11053). · Pico's stance: reports

Doctrine

Farmer's note explains that 'objects of common sense' (or 'common sensibles') comprised everything, like magnitude, perceptible by more than one sense organ, and that scholastic debate concerned how many such objects existed and whether a distinct faculty, the sensus communis, was needed to perceive them; Pico, Farmer adds, 'planned an extended discussion' of the topic (farmer1998:11125). Edelheit finds 'in thesis 10 it is stated that an object per se and especially of the common sense is magnitude, as Avicenna well argued,' tracing this to Albert's commentary on De anima, Book 2, Tractatus 3, Chapter 5, where the sensatum (sensed object) is divided into two kinds (edelheit2022:9639, 9662).

Context

Edelheit again groups this thesis among 7-10, a set on sense perception drawn from Albert (edelheit2022:9488), and reiterates his difficulty in explaining Pico's rationale for devoting several theses to such standard, limited material (edelheit2022:10159).

Historiography

Edelheit (2022) attributes the doctrine to Albert's De anima commentary on the division of the sensatum, citing Pico's wording at 900 Theses, page 214 (edelheit2022:9662).
