Grauia potius seipsis, quam a seipsis mouentur.

Copy-text: Farmer 1998, line 11784

English

Heavy bodies are moved in themselves rather than by themselves. unverified draft

'seipsis' (ablative, 'in themselves') is distinguished from 'a seipsis' (ablative of agency, 'by themselves'); the thesis denies heavy bodies are self-movers while granting the motion is intrinsic to them (farmer1998:11784).

Doctrine

The thesis distinguishes moving 'in themselves' (seipsis) from moving 'by themselves' (a seipsis), denying heavy bodies the stronger status of self-movers; Edelheit reports that Thomas's commentary on Aristotle's Physics, Book 8, Lecture 7, sets out a classification of ways of moving, including per se and a seipso, against which the thesis is to be read, and that on this classification heavy and light bodies 'are moved by an external or generating mover which provides them with form or prohibits anything to withdraw, and thus they do not move themselves like living bodies' (edelheit2022:12514).

Context

Farmer groups this thesis with 2.37 as the first pair in a series on the Aristotelian principle that whatever is moved is moved by another, tracing both to Thomas's Commentary on the Physics 7-8 (farmer1998:11863).

unverified draft

Historiography

Edelheit (2022) reads thesis 38 as completing, alongside thesis 37, Pico's restatement of Thomas's Aristotelian classification of movers, distinguishing bodies moved by an external or generating cause from living bodies that move themselves (edelheit2022:12514).
