Latin

Nouem sunt angelorum hierarchiae_quarum nomina Cherubim, Seraphim, Hasmalim, Haiot, Aralim, Tarsisim, Ophanim, Tephsarim, Isim.

Copy-text: Farmer 1998, line 17536

Apparatus (Farmer): 1486 nomina.Cherubim.Seraphim.Hasmalim ... etc. | 1487 hagot | 1486, 1487 Tephsraim

The underscore in Farmer's transcription ('hierarchiae_quarum') marks an OCR line join, not a word or punctuation mark of the 1486 print; the 1487 apparatus reads 'hagot' for 'Haiot' and both editions read 'Tephsraim' rather than 'Tephsarim' (farmer1998:17552).

English

There are nine hierarchies of angels, whose names are Cherubim, Seraphim, Hasmalim, Haiot, Aralim, Tarsisim, Ophanim, Tephsarim, and Isim. unverified draft

A list of transliterated Hebrew angelic names; rendered here in Pico's own transliteration rather than corrected to any standard Hebrew form, since the names themselves (and their order) are the object of scholarly comment (copenhaver2019:15260).

Doctrine

Pico states that the kabbalistic angelic hierarchy comprises nine ranks rather than the ten more usual in Jewish tradition (copenhaver2019:15260), a departure Farmer's note takes as intended to correlate the scheme with Pseudo-Dionysius's nine orders of Christian angels (farmer1998:17567). Copenhaver identifies a Kabbalist text known to Pico as the source of the particular names used, while observing that Pico altered their sequence from that text (copenhaver2019:15260).

Context

The nine names given here recur, in the same order, in Copenhaver's discussion of the thesis alongside a second, later Pico thesis in the same idiom (copenhaver2019:20042). The 1486 and 1487 print witnesses show minor orthographic variance among the names themselves (farmer1998:17552).

Historiography

Wirszubski (1989) transcribes this conclusio (his 'Conclusio II') from the 1486 print and proposes Flavius Mithridates' Latin translation of the Corona Nominis Boni, by Abraham Axelrad of Cologne, as Pico's likeliest direct source (farmer1998:17567, reporting Wirszubski 1989: 22-23). Farmer's note, however, records that the transliterated angelic names and their order in Pico's thesis 'differ radically' from those found in Mithridates' translation as Farmer there quotes it (farmer1998:17567), leaving the precise relationship between Pico's list and its proposed source unresolved by the material consulted. Copenhaver (2019) instead locates a Kabbalist text, unnamed further in the material consulted, as the source of Pico's names, noting that 'Jewish tradition often preferred ten groups' of angels and that Pico's ordering of the nine names differs from that text's (copenhaver2019:15260).

Pico's sources

  • A Kabbalist text using this nine-fold, differently ordered list of angelic names: Copenhaver identifies a Kabbalist text known to Pico as the origin of these names, noting Pico changed their order (copenhaver2019:15260)
  • Wirszubski's proposed identification of Mithridates' translation of the Corona Nominis as Pico's likeliest direct source: reported by Farmer as Wirszubski's claim (Wirszubski 1989: 22-23), though Farmer notes the transliterations and order in Mithridates' rendering of the Corona Nominis 'differ radically' from Pico's thesis (farmer1998:17567)
