Thesis 6.4

Latin

Pater et filius non solum duo spirantes, sed etiam duo spiratores dici possunt.

Copy-text: Farmer 1998, line 12682

English

The Father and the Son can be called not only two who breathe forth, but also two breathers-forth. unverified draft

Farmer's note explains that 'spirantes' (a participle) and 'spiratores' (a substantive), both from 'spirare', to breathe, were applied to the first two Persons as they gave rise to the procession of the Holy Spirit; Thomas used 'spirantes' in this context but avoided 'spiratores', which he took to imply too strong a distinction between the Persons, unlike Giles (paraphrase, farmer1998:12761).

Translation: original translation, drafted 2026-09-26 by WRITER-full-batch7, from Latin at farmer1998:12682

Attribution: Reports Giles of Rome (farmer1998:12682; edelheit2022:16259). · Pico's stance: reports

Doctrine

Edelheit reports that 'spiration' stands at the centre of the thesis, and that it takes us to Trinitarian theology and to the manner in which plurality can or cannot be attributed to the Persons through participles or adjectives on the one hand, and through nouns on the other (paraphrase, edelheit2022:16044, edelheit2022:16259). He identifies Giles's Reportatio lecturae on the Sentences as the source, and separately quotes Albert the Great's Opera omnia as offering a related but contrasting formula (edelheit2022:16044; edelheit2022:16287).

Context

Farmer's note remarks that the conflict over 'spirantes' and 'spiratores' illustrates the metaphysical importance scholastic traditions could attach to minute grammatical distinctions (paraphrase, farmer1998:12761).
