Where Are the Angels? Place, Motion and Authority in Pico's Trial: A Tour

Guided tour. About 3704 words, 63 cited claims. Quotations are cut to 40 words; each carries its work and line.

This tour follows the one angelological question that reached Pico's judges, and it keeps to six stops (ed.). The first asks why the place of bodiless spirits mattered to a scholastic theologian; the second gathers what the scholars report of Aquinas, Scotus and three contemporaries on place, motion and knowledge; the third takes up the Paris condemnation of 1277 and the argument over its authority; the fourth sets Pico's thesis that Thomas and Scotus do not differ beside his defence of the Apology's first question; the fifth turns to Pedro Garsia's reply and the commission's ruling; the sixth lists what the sources leave open (ed.).

One limit governs every stop (ed.). Neither Aquinas nor Scotus is in the corpus this edition draws on, and neither are Henry of Ghent, Giles of Rome, Godfrey of Fontaines or Durandus; nor are the Apology and Garsia's Determinationes magistrales themselves (ed.). Everything below about those men and those texts is what Edelheit, Copenhaver and Black report, and where Garsia is concerned it is what Edelheit reports Garsia to say (ed.). Where the scholars disagree, both sides are named and neither is settled here (ed.).

Stop 1 — Why the question mattered

Copenhaver poses as the heart of Pico's defence in Q1 the question of what a believer must believe about the descent, given the puzzle of how a disembodied soul goes to a place, one district of Hell rather than another 1. Copenhaver says that Pico studied the localization of angels in order to understand how Christ's soul could have been located in Hell 2. A theory of where angels are was therefore, for Pico, a route to a theory of where a bodiless soul can be (ed.).

Copenhaver reports that the vocabulary evolved to explicate a passage of Peter Lombard's Sentences, which distinguishes being in a place by measurement in length, height and breadth from being directed and delimited to a place, a mode that befits every created spirit 3. Edelheit reports that Pico cites Albert, who refers to philosophers using John of Damascus's double account of being in a place, circumscriptive and definitive, and rejects the definitive account for intelligences and souls 4. Hobbes later mocked the scholastic consensus in the terms that an angel is in a place "not measurably (dimensive) or by enclosure (circumscriptive)" but by delimitation (definitive) 5.

Inside that consensus the dispute concerned what grounds the delimitation (ed.). Copenhaver reports that some insisted angels could have places only by activity, while others countered that an angel which did not act somewhere would be nowhere 6. Edelheit reports a second division, which Garsia drew: the philosophers, bound to Aristotelian astronomy and metaphysics, must hold the intelligences immovable and without attachment to a place, while some theologians hold as a probable opinion that angels and separate souls are attached to a place and move locally in their own substance 7. Edelheit adds that the question of how Christ, a spiritual being, descended into a physical place is the scholastic form of what became the psycho-physical problem of early modern philosophy 8.

Copenhaver counts at least five of Pico's theses, on the eucharist, the incarnation and the descent, for which the placement of bodiless angels and souls was of great interest 9. Pico remembered scriptural texts that seem to give angels a place by describing them as sent, though he held that the biblical message is that angels act here or there where they were not previously active, not that they are in a place 10.

Stop 2 — Aquinas, Scotus and the others, as the scholars report them

Edelheit reports that Pico cited three passages from Thomas's Sentences commentary in support of the view that an angel is determined in a place through its action or operation 11. Copenhaver writes that one response to how a bodiless Gabriel can be in Mary's room was that separated substances can be somewhere through what they do there, and adds that Thomas said something of this kind; the Summa theologiae 3.52.2 is cited in his note, and the passage is not quoted 12. On the descent itself Copenhaver says Thomas taught that Jesus went to Hell both essentially and effectively, and was essentially present only where the Fathers were held 13.

Scotus, in the scholars' account, stands for the other ground of place (ed.). Edelheit reports that the Scotists argue that the finitude and limitation of an essence, and the final intrinsic determination of its substance, is the reason a separated substance exists in a place 14. Scotus, as Copenhaver reports him, agreed that "an angel is often nowhere," since an angel does not act in the empyrean heaven, which cannot change or move 6. Garsia's taxonomy, taken up in Stop 5, assigns definitive presence through the angel's own nature to the Scotists and Nominalists, and a view based on limitation of power rather than essence to the founder of the school 15.

Henry of Ghent appears to be the scholars' most candid witness (ed.). Edelheit reports that Henry advised Étienne Tempier in 1277 on the condemned propositions, which makes him the authority for clarifying proposition 204, the subtext of both Henry's and Pico's discussion of angels in place 16. On how angels are related to a place, Henry admits that he does not know and prefers to hear others 17. Edelheit says that, citing Henry's Quodlibet VII, q. 9 on the double limitation of every created being, by nature and by place, Henry admits he does not know how to explain the difference between created beings and God in relation to place, the problem being how an angel's presence or activity in a place differs from God's 18. Edelheit suggests that Henry's honest, non-dogmatic approach might have been very appealing to Pico, whose own approach was non-dogmatic and inclusive 19.

Motion and knowledge come through other authors. Edelheit says the source of Pico's thesis 11, that if something moves in a vacuum it moves instantaneously, is Giles of Rome's Reportatio 1, q. 6, on whether the movement of an angel is measured by time, where a reference to Averroes on movement in a vacuum leads to the conclusion that an angel's movement is instantaneous; the thesis, he adds, does not exactly reflect Giles's view but his reference to Averroes 20. Giles also taught, on Edelheit's reading, that the angelic intellect is superior to ours because it understands through a more universal species, so that a superior angel needs fewer species than an inferior one 21. Black reports that, according to Pico, Dionysius and Thomas both hold angelic intelligence superior to human because it comprehends with fewer forms 22. Edelheit finds in Thomas's Quodlibet 5, q. 4 a hierarchy of degrees even among angels of one order, which he suggests might be the origin of Pico's angelus beatior 23. Godfrey of Fontaines, in Copenhaver's phrase, studied with Henry but followed Aquinas as much as Henry 24.

Stop 3 — The Paris articles of 1277 and their authority

Étienne Tempier, Bishop of Paris, condemned in 1277 more than two hundred detestable errors, and his backers were theologians worried about Aristotelian natural philosophy as Averroes and other Muslims interpreted it 25. Some of the articles concerned angels 12. Edelheit gives article 218 in Latin, "Quod intelligentia, angelus, vel anima separata nusquam est," and reports that Pico set it against article 204, on separated substances being somewhere by operation 26. Copenhaver notes that Tempier and Godfrey of Fontaines are both named in Q1 although the Conclusions never mention them 27.

Pico's argument had two parts (ed.). Edelheit reports that Pico tries to show article 218 supports his position no less than article 204 opposes it, so that the two articles contradict each other 26. Copenhaver adds that Godfrey, examining the 1277 statements on locating angels, concluded that they and several other articles plainly cannot go with one another, and that Pico, citing Godfrey, said Tempier's articles "need much correction since some are false and some contradict themselves" 24. The second part concerns reach: Edelheit reports Pico's claim that the articles do not oblige all believers unless they rest on Scripture or on decisions of the universal Church, and his joke that as the English say the articles do not cross the sea, Italians could say they do not cross the Alps 28.

Garsia answers on both points (ed.). Edelheit says Garsia refers to the joke Pico mentions, that the Parisian condemnation does not cross the sea or the Alps, and presents Scotus's response, with which he strongly agrees; Edelheit adds that using the 1277 condemnation as a criterion means Thomas can no longer be regarded as the common doctor on every matter 29. Copenhaver supplies background: Aquinas was attacked repeatedly in the half-century between the condemnations of 1277 and his canonization in 1323 30.

Two recorded disputes follow (ed.). The first concerns the authority of the articles. Pico, with Godfrey behind him, treats some of them as false or self-contradictory and in need of correction 24; Garsia treats the same articles as a criterion, and Edelheit reports that Garsia concludes that the third, fourth and fifth views of angelic place should be condemned as "erroneous, false and indicative of heresy" 31. The second concerns whether the question was still open. Edelheit says Henry's recurrent refusal to determine the right explanation was exactly what Pico needed against the commission, because it makes the matter "a matter of theological speculation only, not of the necessary truth of faith" 32. By Garsia's criterion only the views of the Scotists and Nominalists and of Scotus remain acceptable 31, so for him the question was closed against Pico (ed.).

Stop 4 — Pico's concord thesis and the Apology's first question

Among the theses that open the Conclusions, Aquinas received forty-five of the 115 assigned to the six Latin philosophers and theologians, and Scotus twenty-two 33. Pico cited a thesis, which was not condemned, saying "that Thomas and Scotus do not differ about angels on their manner of being in a place" 34. The condemned thesis on the descent said that Christ did not truly descend to Hell as far as real presence is concerned, as proposed by Thomas and on the usual approach, but only in regard to the effect; Copenhaver reports that Pico's usual approach was the one followed by Scotus, Bonaventura, the Scotists and almost all nominalists, and that Pico believed Thomas held it too 35. Edelheit warns that because Scotists and Nominalists are cited together here, the phrases via antiqua and via moderna should be used with caution for fifteenth-century terms 36.

In the Apology, Pico's own opinion, as Edelheit reports it, is that only Christ's soul descended to Hell, which leads to the status of separate souls in relation to place, with presence identified with operation 37. Edelheit adds that on Pico's argument an incorporeal substance is present in a place because of its operation or effect, just as a corporeal substance is present because of its quantity and not its substantiality 14. Edelheit says Pico contends that an incorporeal thing is completely abstracted from a physical place, unlike a point, which has a place by its own nature; he finds the sequence of Pico's argument before the explicit reference similar to Henry's, though Henry's emphasis on simplicity is missing 38. Of Pico's list of authorities, Edelheit says Henry supports only the first part of the view, that angels are not related to a place through their incorporeal substance 39. Pico also invoked Durandus, whom, Edelheit reports, Pico presents as having held the effect-only account probable and defensible together with the article of faith 40.

A dispute about Thomas. Edelheit reports Pico saying in the Apology that when he advanced the conclusion he believed this was Thomas's opinion, as his conclusion that Thomas and Scotus do not differ on the angels' mode of being in a place shows, and that to err about Thomas's opinion is not to err in faith 41. Edelheit says that, as Pico sees it, Thomas is inconsistent: in the Sentences 1 he seems to agree with Pico's view, while in Sentences 4 and mainly the third part of the Summa he tends to agree with the Scotists that Christ was in Hell through his essence 42. Edelheit says it is clear that for Pico Thomas is not an authority but one source among many, and that Pico points out inconsistencies "not to show that Thomas is wrong, but to show that all such opinions are only probable" 4243. Garsia takes the opposite view of the same Summa passage: he reads 3, q. 52 as assuming that an angel is in a place through operation, as a separated soul is, and as giving a definitive answer, without any contradiction, that Christ's soul truly descended in the real presence of his substance, though Edelheit notes that this explanation cannot be found in Thomas 44. So Pico finds Thomas in tension with himself, Garsia finds him consistent, and Edelheit finds Garsia's consistency supplied from outside the text (ed.).

A dispute about Pico's own position. Copenhaver reports Pico's conclusion that a separated substance is present as to place by delimitation (definitive), quotes Pico as saying that on this point his approach agrees with Scotus and all the Scotists, and adds that Pico held it befitting for Christ's soul to have been in Hell only by the effect and by acting 45. Edelheit's 2008 account calls the second explanation Pico presents that of Scotus and the Scotists, which Pico prefers, on which an incorporeal substance may truly be in a place definitively though its reason for being there is its operation 46. His 2022 account draws a line inside the agreement: Pico's view accords with Scotus and the Scotists so far, and differs where the Scotists make the finitude and limitation of an essence the reason for presence 14. The 2008 account calls the Scotist explanation the one Pico prefers 46, while the 2022 account has Pico reject the Scotist explanation of why a separated substance is in a place 14; the two are not merged here (ed.). Garsia, as Edelheit reports him, sorts the views into five, and the fifth, distinct from the Scotist first view, "is the one defended by Pico" 15. Edelheit's 2008 account places Pico somewhere between the Thomists and the Scotists 47. Whether Pico's position is the Scotist one, as Copenhaver's report of Pico's own words suggests, or a distinct position, as Edelheit and Garsia's taxonomy suggest, is a recorded difference between the accounts that the sources do not reconcile (ed.).

What each scholar says Pico was doing. Copenhaver suggests that Pico shifted the argument from Hell to heavenly messengers after a few paragraphs, that Christ may have looked a riskier topic than angels, and that "angels were safer substitutes for Christ in Pico's metaphysical inquiry"; the motives, Copenhaver adds, are easy to imagine, and the passage does not have Pico stating them 2. Questions about angels, much discussed by philosophers, "were surrogates in Q1 for a less examined problem about bodiless spatiality," and Kabbalah was not on the table for Q1 4849. Copenhaver says further that all the condemned theses except Q5 on Kabbalah and Q7 on Origen are scholastic through and through 50. The scope of these statements is Q1, where angels serve as stand-ins in a scholastic argument about place, and Q5, which Copenhaver sets outside the scholastic questions (ed.). The claims consulted contain no statement by Copenhaver about a Dionysian hierarchy of angels in the Apology, and none about angelology across all the Questions (not established by the sources consulted) (ed.).

Edelheit's account has a different emphasis. He reports Pico's own summary of his conclusion: because only Christ's soul descended, and souls are separated and not compatible with space, it seems he did not descend by local movement but only through his operation, so that without physical presence his soul was present in Hell and produced the effects 51. He notes that in the defence it was in Pico's interest to appear as little original as possible 52. Copenhaver's account bears on why Pico turned to angels 2, and Edelheit's on what Pico argued and how he framed it 5152 (ed.).

Stop 5 — Garsia's answer and the commission's stance

Edelheit describes Pedro Garsia as the Spanish bishop of Barcelona and a member of the papal commission that condemned thirteen theses, who wrote the Determinationes magistrales at the request of Innocent VIII in response to the Apology 53. Under interrogation Pico insisted that no one should understand him to say that Christ's soul did not descend into Hell; his judges nonetheless ruled the Q1 thesis, strictly speaking and given the meaning of its words, false, erroneous, heretical and contrary to sacred scripture, and recorded the verdict on 5 March 1487 54.

Garsia sorts the theological views of angelic place into five: the Scotists and Nominalists, the founder of the Scotist school, the Thomists (operation), an effect-only view, and a fifth that he says Pico follows 15. On Edelheit's report the fifth view agrees with the fourth in denying that formally and subjectively local movement and definite presence accord with the angel's nature, but adds that the angel is present in any body it can move, so that its presence precedes its operation, a priority Edelheit relates to Durandus 55. Garsia analyses Durandus at length as the first author and defender of the conclusion Pico presented, which divests Pico of any claim of originality 52.

Garsia's own account is the definitive one: separate souls and angels, being of limited and finite nature, are in a determined place definitively and indivisibly without being circumscribed by it, a solution he says can be proved by reasons and authorities though not by demonstration 56. Edelheit reports that Garsia grades the other views: the philosophers' view is simply heretical according to Thomas; the second theological view is more probable and more in agreement with biblical authorities and the saints; the third is suspected of error and heresy because an angel's operation cannot be material and so cannot attach it to a place; and the fourth and fifth are most suspect because they favour the philosophers' view, above all the fifth, on which angels are present everywhere 57.

Edelheit says Garsia takes Durandus's view as part of the scholastic method of raising theoretical problems, and holds that where the real meaning of terms and propositions is at stake one must follow the common way; according to Garsia, Pico in defending his theses challenges the authority of the common way on behalf of views that seem to him more probable 58. Garsia identifies the common way with Thomas, citing Giles of Rome, Henry of Ghent, Godfrey of Fontaines, Scotus and Peter Auriol as calling him the common doctor, and treats Pico's effort to blur the common way with the views of Scotus and the Nominalists as ridiculous 59. Edelheit sums up that Garsia criticises what he regards as Pico's Nominalist views, which he associates with Scotus and his followers, and that there was real disagreement about how the common way should be defined 60. Edelheit also records a tension: the common way is Thomas's, yet the views Garsia's 1277 criterion leaves acceptable are those of Scotus and the Nominalists, so that the Thomistic third view is itself condemned, and Garsia does not seem to be aware of it 61.

Stop 6 — What remains open

The sources leave five questions undecided, and the sources are the limit of what can be said here (ed.).

Whether Thomas is consistent on angelic place is the first. Pico, as Edelheit reports, holds that he is not 42, and Garsia reads the Summa 3, q. 52 passage as giving a definitive answer without any contradiction 44. Neither Aquinas's text nor Pico's is in the corpus, so the disagreement cannot be adjudicated from the material consulted (not established by the sources consulted) (ed.).

Whether the place question was open or closed by 1277 is the second. Edelheit reports Pico's use of Henry to show it was open 32, and Garsia's use of the articles to rule out the views that include Pico's 31. Copenhaver notes that Godfrey argued, in a quodlibetal question on angels, that a question so unsettled that beliefs may vary without risk to faith should not be chained to one view, and reads this as "debate open in the scholastic way, where fides always came first" 62. Godfrey would therefore support Pico's claim of openness only within limits Copenhaver draws (ed.).

Whether Pico's position is Scotist or distinct is the third, and the accounts of Copenhaver, Edelheit and Garsia in Stop 4 stand side by side 4514. A related point sits inside Edelheit's report of Garsia: the fifth view makes the angel's presence precede its operation 55, while Edelheit's account of Pico's own summary identifies presence with operation 37. Whether Garsia's fifth view renders Pico's stated view accurately is not settled by the claims consulted (ed.). Edelheit adds that Henry supports only the first part of Pico's view 39, and that Pico seems to push Henry towards his own position while Henry stays cautious and uncommitted 17.

The figures for the articles differ. Copenhaver says Tempier condemned more than two hundred 25, and Edelheit speaks of 219 condemned propositions in one passage 16 and of 220 in another 31. The sources consulted do not say whether the figures count the same things (not established by the sources consulted) (ed.).

The last question is Pico's motive for turning from Hell to the angels. Copenhaver reads the shift as prophylactic and practical and marks it as inference 2. Edelheit suggests that Pico may have been drawn to Henry's candour 19, and Pico offers a list of authorities that Henry supports only in part 39. Neither account reports Pico announcing a reason (ed.).

Copenhaver's judgment on the whole affair is that, drawing on advanced medieval theories, Pico aligned himself with venerable beliefs that Hobbes and his contemporaries eventually abandoned, so that the project of the Conclusions and the Apology did not look to the future 63.
