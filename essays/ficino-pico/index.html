Ficino and Pico on the Metaphysics of the One: A Tour

Guided tour. About 4323 words, 84 cited claims. Quotations are cut to 40 words; each carries its work and line.

This is a tour, not a single argument, and it keeps to six stops (ed.). The first two stops lay background: how the Dionysian corpus, which scholars associate with apophatic theology, reached the Latin West, and what Aquinas and then Ficino made of it, as the scholars cited report (ed.). Neither Thomas Aquinas nor Pseudo-Dionysius is himself in the corpus this edition draws on: no Summa and no Corpus Dionysiacum has been mined for claims here, so everything said about either man below is what the scholars cited, or Pico himself, report or say about them, not a reading of their own pages (ed.). The third stop walks through Pico's De ente et uno in the order of its own chapters (ed.). The fourth asks where, and how directly, that treatise takes aim at Marsilio Ficino, and represents a live scholarly disagreement on both sides rather than settling it (ed.). The fifth follows the treatise into the exchange with Antonio Cittadini (ed.). The sixth does not close the tour but leaves it open, on questions the sources themselves leave open (ed.).

Stop 1 — Dionysius in the Latin West and Aquinas's encounter with him

Black describes the corpus as "a group of religious works written in Greek by an unknown author in the late fifth or early sixth century and heavily influenced by the later Neoplatonists" 1. Copenhaver, discussing Dionysius's use of the verb perichorein, calls the language emphatically Platonist and says Dionysius was a contemporary of Proclus 2. Allen, in one place, calls him "Proclus' great sixth century disciple" 3. Black adds that the corpus was attributed to Dionysius the Areopagite, the figure converted by Paul, that it "was believed to date from the second half of the first century," and that it comprises four treatises and ten letters 1.

Ficino, whose late reading of Dionysius matters for the next stop, believed, on Allen's report, that the Areopagite was "the authentic Athenian disciple of St. Paul and the crown (culmen) of Christian Platonism" 4. Allen sets this against his own correction that the man was in fact Proclus's disciple, not Paul's (ed.). Black reports that "in 1489 Dionysius was still, by and large, the Areopagite; the criticism of Valla had yet to take effect; and Marsilio Ficino was about to produce his own translations of part of the corpus—the second translation produced in Florence in the fifteenth century" 5. Howlett's account of the medieval transmission says that the author of the corpus became influential in Latin Europe in the twelfth century, influencing Aquinas and mystics such as Meister Eckhart, and became even more popular through Ambrogio Traversari's 1436 translation 6 — though the same passage calls that wider popularity a fourteenth-century phenomenon while dating the translation itself to 1436, a discrepancy taken up again in Stop 6 (ed.).

What Black reports about the Neoplatonic background of Aquinas's reading is set out in the sentences that follow (ed.). Black reports that Proclus's Elements of Theology passed into Arabic and then into Latin as the Liber de causis, attributed to Aristotle until the thirteenth century, when William of Moerbeke's 1268 translation of the Elements let Thomas Aquinas see that the Liber de causis was based on Proclus's work 7. The next sentences give what Black, Howlett and Edelheit report of Aquinas's use of the Areopagite (ed.). Black's footnote quotes Aquinas, at the opening of the Summa theologiae, as saying on Dionysius's authority that the new law is "figura futurae gloriae" 8. Schmitt's judgment, as Howlett reports it, is that Aquinas was primarily Aristotelian in form but Platonic in his metaphysics 9; separately, Black says the idea of "mutual containment" entered the Christian tradition through Pseudo-Dionysius and that both the Divine Names and Aquinas's commentary on it develop it 10. On angels specifically, Edelheit finds Aquinas, in Quodlibet 5, q. 4, following Pseudo-Dionysius closely on the hierarchy among angels even of the same order, and suggests this might be the origin of Pico's notion of an angelus beatior 11.

Where Miller and Sudduth locate Aquinas's importance for what follows is on the question that occupies the whole of this tour: whether God is being, or above it (ed.). Miller states flatly that "there can be no doubt that Pico derived his metaphysic of existence from Thomas" 12, and says of the passage on being in the second sense that Pico is there utilizing the metaphysics of Aquinas, the first Christian philosopher to base his metaphysics on the real distinction between that which participates existence, ens, and the act of existing itself, ipsum esse 13. Sudduth reads Pico's God in exactly these terms, as reproducing "the Thomistic position that God is ipsum esse, the act of existence of itself" 14. Howlett notes that Aquinas in the thirteenth century attempted to marry Aristotelianism with Christianity, while Ficino, following Augustine, built his union of faith and reason on Platonism and Christianity 15. Sudduth describes Pico's view of God as reproducing the Thomistic ipsum esse 14, and Miller says Pico there utilizes Aquinas's distinction of ens and esse 13, while Howlett quotes De ente as placing God above being and dwelling in darkness 16; the treatise holds both (ed.).

Stop 2 — Ficino's position

Ficino's own view of the Parmenides, on Howlett's account, held to "Platonic orthodoxy, deriving from Plotinus, often filtered through Proclus, that Plato argues the One is above Being" 17. Miller's introduction to the Wallis translation reports that for Ficino, following Plotinus and Proclus, "Plato's dialectical form is only an outer covering for a profound metaphysical content" 18. Miller adds that Ficino's reading gained added authority from Proclus's commentary on the Parmenides, which breaks off at the end of the first hypothesis, so that a reader who relies on that commentary more than on the dialogue's eight hypotheses tends to take those texts as Plato's last and most profound words on the one 19.

Allen reports that Ficino saw the via negativa as running from the Parmenides to the Areopagite 4, and describes the shape Ficino gave that apophaticism in his commentary on the Mystical Theology: a sevenfold contemplative ascent by subtraction, banishing in turn body, corporeal passions, imagination's images, reason's discursive arguments, intellect's multiformity, and finally the condition of being either soul or intellect, until "we will always find the absolute Unity itself, that is, God, lying concealed beneath our own soul's particular unity" 20. Howlett notes that where Ficino and Pico deviate, Ficino emphasizes one God rather than "the Proclan baroque universe of henads," whereas Pico's Kabbalism allows him to explore Proclus in all his complexity 21.

Miller says Ficino, the first Renaissance thinker to know all of Plato's original writings, continued to read him within the Neoplatonic tradition, and that although Pico's interpretation differs in some respects from Ficino's, both follow the long medieval tradition of the concord of Plato and Aristotle 22. Edelheit draws the boundary of that shared ground more narrowly: for all that Plotinus and Proclus figure largely among the nine hundred theses, Pico, on his reading, "is in no way a Platonist like Ficino," and it seems, on Edelheit's view, that he offers even their opinions as further opinions to be examined 23. It is this difference in temperament toward a shared inheritance, Ficino's adherence to the Plotinian-Proclan orthodoxy on the One above being against what Edelheit takes to be Pico's treatment of Plotinus and Proclus as further opinions to be examined, that the next stop's treatise puts under strain (ed.).

Stop 3 — De ente et uno, argument by argument

Pico gives his own occasion for the treatise in its opening lines, addressed to Poliziano: Lorenzo de' Medici had lately disputed against Aristotle on being and the one, "supported by the reasons of the Platonists" 24. Pico calls his own contribution to that discussion no more than a confirmation of what Poliziano had already answered Lorenzo 25, written up at Poliziano's own request as a brief compendium, with a fuller treatment promised in a projected Concord of Plato and Aristotle 26. Pico's own description of his philosophy follows at once, in his own words: "those who think that Aristotle disagrees with Plato disagree with me, who make a concordant philosophy of both" 27. Chapter one states the disagreement to be resolved: Aristotle holds in many places that the one and being, and likewise the true and the good, "correspond to each other and are the same in extent," where the Academy opposes this and holds the one prior to being 28.

Chapter two turns to the two Platonic dialogues the Academy relies on. Pico finds that Plato disputed being and the one in exactly two places, the Parmenides and the Sophist, and that the Academics read both passages as placing the one above being 29. Against the Parmenides he presses a distinction between a hypothesis and a doctrine: even granting that the dialogue conveys doctrine rather than mere dialectical exercise, it still matters whether Plato asserts outright that the one is above being, or only the conditional that if all things were one, that one would not be being 30. He then grants the stronger reading anyway, for argument's sake, "let us grant that Plato affirmed something which he certainly nowhere affirmed," in order to ask in what sense such a claim, even if made, could be true 31. Against the Sophist he reads the text more simply, as making the one and being equal rather than the one superior, so that "Plato held it as certain that the one was being" 32.

Chapter three brings a third ancient authority against the Academy's reading. If we credit Simplicius and Parmenides's other defenders, Pico argues, Parmenides the Pythagorean meant God, not the cosmos, when he said that what is, is one — so the one cannot be above being unless it is above God 33. Pico reaches for Dionysius in the same chapter: those who dispute against him "make into a patron of their opinion" a Dionysius who, Pico insists, will not deny that God said to Moses "I am who am" 34.

Chapter four supplies the technical apparatus the whole argument depends on. Being can be taken in two senses: as all that is outside nothing, the sense in which Aristotle made being equal to the one 35, and in a second sense on which it is nevertheless true that something may be placed above the eminence of being 36. That second sense rests on a distinction of grammar mapped onto metaphysics, between concrete names, which "signify... that which is such not from itself, but by the gift of another," and abstract names, which signify what is such from itself 37. Being (ens) is concrete, on this scheme; existence (esse) is its abstract form, and whatever participates existence is called being, "just as that which participates light... is called luminous" 38. God alone is not a participant in this sense but existence itself, of and from himself, the source of all things with no interposing medium — and for exactly this reason Pico concludes that God is not being but above being, and, since the title one belongs to God, that he may equally be called the one above being 3940. Pico argues that Aristotle too places God above being: Aristotle divides being into being per se, which falls under the ten categories, and accidental being, and no good interpreter doubts that God escapes the first sort, a reading Pico credits to Aquinas's commentary on the Sentences 41. Some Platonists, he adds, boast without warrant of a mystery unknown to Aristotle in calling God one and good prior to being, when the Peripatetics already know how to place God above being in their own terms 42. Pico argues that if Plato does affirm in the first hypothesis of the Parmenides that the one is higher than being, that one is nothing other than God, "as even the Academics admit" 43.

Chapter five turns the same distinction against the Platonists directly: Pico sets out to refute their arguments that the one is superior to being, since they hold this "not in the sense in which we also agree with it, but in an absolute sense, against Aristotle" 44; the quarrel, that is, is not over whether God is above being, which Pico has already granted in his own vocabulary, but over the absolute sense the Platonists give the thesis (ed.). The chapter's discussion of the angels notes that their life is not perfect, since "unless the vivifying ray of divine light constantly warmed it, it would all fall into nothingness" 45; separately, Pico adds that if intellect is taken as a nature tending outside itself to the intelligible as to another thing, Aristotle no less than the Platonists will deny that God is intellect and intelligible 46. Howlett quotes the chapter's own account of the ascent this reasoning builds toward: having advanced, by purging the divine names of every imperfection, to the point of saying that God is above being, above true, above one and above good, "since he is existence itself, truth itself, unity itself, goodness itself... we are still in light. God, however, has established his dwelling in darkness" 16. Pico's own text then anchors this darkness in Dionysius by name, citing him as the author who, after all he wrote in his other treatises, "finally at the end of that work spoke as he could about God in a very holy way, as if he were already in the cloud" 47.

The middle chapters work through consequences. Chapter six takes up prime matter, which is "neither altogether without unity nor without being," receiving its precise unity from the same form from which it receives being — a further dispute, over whether that unity is affirmative or negative, Pico declines to adjudicate as already well known to any student of Aristotle 48.

Chapter eight adds a scholastic refinement from Avicenna's followers, who further divide the one into one and something, and being into being and thing, a division Pico judges to raise little disagreement and no real conflict with Plato's own enumeration in the Sophist 49. It then restates the chapter's convertibility thesis in its simplest form: give a thing being, Pico writes, "and it is certainly also one. Who does not say one, says nothing, as Plato says in the Sophist" 50.

Chapter nine works out how the four transcendental names, being, one, true and good, order themselves in God taken absolutely against God taken as cause. Pico says the four are not in God in relation to any cause, since he, the cause of all things, is from nothing, and adds that they may be considered in God either absolutely or as cause, a distinction that does not apply to creatures, since God is able not to be a cause 51. Considered as cause, the order reverses, so that God is understood first as one, then good, then true, and finally being, because the cause called the end is prior to the exemplary cause, and the exemplary cause prior to the efficient cause 52.

Chapter ten closes the treatise on the human stakes of this metaphysics. Blum's essay quotes Pico's own closing conclusion: "If these three, one, true, and good, follow by perpetual connection, it follows that when we are not these three, we absolutely are not, even though we may appear to be; and although we may be thought to live, yet we would be ever dying rather than living" 53. Pico reads this practically, as a call to bind sense to reason by the union of virtue, lest we become, like a kingdom with two rival princes reigning by turns, utterly desolated (ed.); the chapter's own image is of following God with the law of mind for a time and Baal with the law of the flesh for a time 54.

Miller's summary of what the treatise as a whole accomplishes states plainly what kind of resolution this is (ed.). Miller holds that Plato's and Aristotle's philosophies, impossible to reconcile on their own terms, can be made to coincide only when transformed into a different philosophy in which the highest principle of each is identified with a God who is existence itself, so that Pico's concord "has been accomplished by transforming both into a different metaphysics" 55 — neither simply Aristotle's own metaphysics nor simply the Academy's, but Pico's (ed.).

Stop 4 — Pico's moves against Ficino

Whether that transformation was aimed, even implicitly, at Ficino by name is where the scholarship divides, and honestly (ed.). Howlett states the strong case flatly: De ente "was also a response to Ficino's writing on Being and the One" 56, already partly an attack on a scholar who had written on being and the one before his own Parmenides commentary and had consistently held the orthodox Platonic view that placed the two apart 57. Allen reports that Pico's dismissal of the Parmenides and the Sophist as dialectical games rejected two texts especially revered by Proclus and by Ficino, "who was annoyed at what he called the count's 'temerity' in dismissing their ontology" 58. Allen states that Pico scholars have failed to understand the subtlety of Ficino's analyses of the Parmenides and Sophist and hence Ficino's role in the generation of De ente and the reaction to it, and refers readers to his own article "The second Ficino-Pico controversy" 59. Howlett dates the counter-reply between 1492 and 1494, while Pico still lived: a commentary that "follows a sequence that specifically takes on Pico's arguments section by section to refute him" 60. Miller likewise reports that Ficino, in his commentary on the Parmenides, replies that Pico's interpretation is "against the opinion of all the Platonists" 61.

Edelheit reads the same treatise differently. Pico, on his account, does not mention Ficino at all in his composition, and criticizes instead the extra-dogmatic Neoplatonic reading associated with the successors of Syrianus, a school from which Ficino, as Allen himself shows, tries to distinguish his own middle path, on which the Parmenides holds both an eristic and a theological aspect 62. Where Howlett calls De ente partly an attack on Ficino, Edelheit reads the proem as implicitly faulting Lorenzo's reliance on the Platonists and as anticipating an implicit criticism of Ficino developed chiefly in the second chapter 63. The two readings stand as a recorded and genuine dispute: Howlett states flatly that the work was a response to Ficino's writing; Edelheit reads the criticism as only implicit in the proem and developed chiefly in chapter two (ed.).

Borghesi, unlike Edelheit, names Ficino among the disputants (ed.). Pico's proem does not name Ficino 62; Borghesi, however, describes the occasion as a dispute, in which Poliziano had asked Pico to intervene, between "the Platonists – in the persons of Lorenzo de' Medici and Marsilio Ficino – and the Peripatetics" 64, a naming of Ficino that Edelheit's account of the proem, with only Lorenzo and Poliziano as disputants, does not make 65 (ed.).

Whatever the proem's own reticence, Allen reports that Ficino was annoyed at Pico's dismissal of the two dialogues 58 (ed.). Howlett reports that Plato is normally understood to posit that the One is above Being, particularly in the Parmenides, differing from Aristotle, who places the two together 66. Howlett says that to reach concord Pico needed to remove several obstacles in Plato, particularly the Parmenides, which he argues is "simply a rhetorical exercise, meaning that we should not take Plato at his word" 67.

The dispute did not end with the Parmenides. Howlett reports Lohr's argument that Pico is attacking Ficino through Aquinas's distinction between these different types of being 68. This is, on Howlett's account, the distinction Pico consistently makes between formal being and causal being, the latter suggesting Aristotle's First Cause and being the cause of the abstract concept of being 69.

Howlett argues that this distinction is at odds with Ficino's understanding of Platonism, and quotes Pico's Commentary on a canzone of Benivieni, where he recalls that a distinguished Platonist "was much astonished at a statement by Plotinus, where he says that God understands nothing and knows nothing" 70. Allen reports the same remark and glosses the great Platonist whose amazement Pico challenges as clearly Ficino 71. Whether or not De ente's own proem names its target, Allen reads a different text of Pico's, the Commentary on a canzone of Benivieni, as answering an unnamed great Platonist's astonishment at Plotinus, whom Allen glosses as clearly Ficino 71 (ed.).

Stop 5 — Cittadini's attack and the aftermath

De ente et uno also drew a critic on the Aristotelian side (ed.). Howlett reports that Pico's polemic was contested from the Platonic side by Ficino and, by his friend the Aristotelian Antonio de Cittadini, from the other 72. Gianfrancesco's Vita of his uncle mentions Cittadini as the only critic named there, calling him an "excellent philosopher"; Gianfrancesco felt obliged to answer Cittadini's fourth objection, to which his uncle had not responded, which Edelheit thinks was "probably because he lost interest in this debate or because of his untimely death" 73. Edelheit reads Pico's first reply to Cittadini as opening by thanking him for his criticism and as admitting, albeit implicitly, that the account of being and the one focuses on converting the concepts into one another, the title to be read together with the proem, which gives the treatise its focus 74. Edelheit reads one point in the exchange, a dispute about how to cite Simplicius, as exposing two very different methods: Pico insists on "maximum precision in any reference, citation or interpretation," where Cittadini is content to follow Simplicius "via Thomas," since for him the argument, not the reference, is what matters 75.

The personal relations around this scholarly friction, on Edelheit's evidence, were cordial (ed.). Cittadini and Gianfrancesco Pico later corresponded with mutual respect and cordial relations, and Gianfrancesco recalled that Cittadini and his uncle had been bound before the disputation itself as compatres, joined by a spiritual bond, in Gianfrancesco's own Latin "spiritali astricti nodo" 76.

The dispute nonetheless outlasted the Apology by years. Copenhaver notes that five years after that defense Pico "was still battling with Antonio Cittadini, a scholastic professor who picked a fight with him about his unfinished treatise On Being and the One" 77. The same passage says Gianfrancesco tried to present his uncle as a saintly Thomist, in line with their shared admiration for Savonarola, whereas leading witnesses in the Apology were theologians whose views were notoriously anti-Thomist 77.

Howlett notes that the treatise's conflict has been discussed often in modern scholarship, citing Van Haelen, Allen and Salas, the last of whom "has taken apart some of the inconsistencies in Pico's argumentation" 78 — a judgment, that is, that the treatise's own arguments do not all sit easily together, made not only by a modern reader but already pressed in the secondary literature (ed.).

Stop 6 — What remains open

Several questions this tour has walked past deserve to be named as open rather than settled, because the sources themselves leave them so (ed.). The most basic is chronological. Miller reports that Ficino's commentary on the Parmenides, replying to Pico, was begun in 1492, the same year as Pico's treatise 61, whereas Edelheit calls the work "De ente et uno of 1491" 79; the two dates stand against each other, and the sources consulted here do not reconcile them (ed.).

Bound up with this is a second, substantive question rather than a merely bibliographic one: whether Ficino's Parmenides commentary came before or after De ente, and so whether it could have been the treatise's occasion or only its answer (ed.). Howlett's own hedge is instructive here: Ficino, she writes, "had probably not yet written a commentary when Pico wrote On Being and the One," publishing it only in 1496 80; Miller's note dates the beginning of that commentary to 1492 61 (ed.).

A third question, which Stop 4 above has already tried to represent as a live dispute rather than settle, is whether De ente et uno attacks Ficino at all, and if so how directly: Howlett's flat identification of the work as a response to Ficino's writing stands recorded against Edelheit's reading of the same proem as criticizing Lorenzo's reliance on the Platonists, with any criticism of Ficino himself only implicit and developed later (ed.). A fourth, closely related question turns the same dispute on a different point of originality (ed.). Edelheit reports that Allen concludes, against Klibansky, that "Ficino emerges... as a good deal more original than Pico" in interpreting the Parmenides 81. Edelheit himself argues that Allen is so intent on a sharp contrast between Pico and Ficino that he does not see their similarity, and that the evidence shows the originality of both 82.

A fifth question belongs to the treatise's own internal architecture rather than to its target (ed.). Howlett asks where the Plotinian One is: "is it Keter or the Ein-Sof?" 83, a question posed in Kabbalistic terms (ed.).

Elsewhere she notes that Pico's second argument, that God is not being but above it, "apparently contradicts his first" argument, that being and the one are equals 84 (ed.).

Finally, on the corpus's own transmission, a smaller but genuine crux remains unresolved in the sources consulted here: Howlett says Pseudo-Dionysius became even more popular in "the fourteenth century" in the very sentence that dates the translation carrying that popularity, Traversari's, to 1436, a fifteenth-century year, without saying which date is the error 6. None of these cruxes is manufactured for the sake of having something to report: each is either flagged by the scholars themselves as unsettled, or produced by setting two claims from the same corpus, or the same book, against one another (ed.). That, and not a tidy conclusion, is where this tour of Ficino, Pico, and the metaphysics of the one currently has to stop (ed.).
