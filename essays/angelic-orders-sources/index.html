The Angelic Orders and Where They Come From: A Tour

Guided tour. About 3853 words, 84 cited claims. Quotations are cut to 40 words; each carries its work and line.

This is a tour of one question, what Pico means when he speaks of orders of angels, in six stops (ed.). The first two follow the Dionysian ladder of seraphim, cherubim and thrones through the Oration and the Heptaplus (ed.). The third turns to the nine Hebrew hierarchies of the Conclusiones and the disagreement over whether Pico turned nine into ten; the fourth takes up Enoch, Metatron and Pallas and the two places where a Hebrew word is missing from the printed text; the fifth asks whether the sefirot, which are emanations and not angels, stand behind the Heptaplus's three worlds, where Black and Wirszubski part company; the sixth asks which Kabbalistic books Pico could have read and lists what the sources leave open (ed.). Where the claims disagree the tour gives both sides and does not choose (ed.). It prints no table matching sefirot to Dionysian orders: a table of that kind in this project's earlier working files was found on audit to have no source, and it is not restored here (ed.).

Stop 1 — The Dionysian ladder in the Oration

In the Oration Pico bids his hearers fly to the otherworldly court near the most eminent Divinity, where "seraphim, cherubim, and thrones occupy the first places", and to emulate their dignity and glory 1. The editors of the Latin-English edition add that these three are known as the first hierarchy because they are closest to God 2, and that Pico here follows the scheme of Pseudo-Dionysius, which Thomas and Gregory confirm 3. Men may live the life the highest angels live, Pico says, and so need not be inferior to them 4.

He gives each rank a character: "The seraph burns with the fire of love; the cherub shines with the splendour of intelligence; the throne stands in the steadfastness of judgment" 5. The cherub sits between the other two, and Pico says that he "uses his light both to prepare us for the seraphic fire" and to illuminate the judgment of the thrones 6. Pico asks how anyone can either judge or love things unknown, and gives Moses as the example who loved what he had seen and judged as a contemplator 7. Pico calls the cherub the bond of the Prime Minds, the order of Pallas and the overseer of contemplative philosophy, and says he is to be emulated first so that we may be raised to love and descend to action 8. The editors gloss the Prime Minds as the angels and the Order of Pallas as a phrase for the cherubim in so far as they preside over contemplators, and caution that calling the cherubim contemplators does not mean the seraphim and thrones are not 910.

Copenhaver describes the Dionysian arrangement at the top of the hierarchy as Thrones cleansing sinners, Cherubs enlightening them and Seraphs perfecting them 11. Black reads the cherubic process of being cleansed, illuminated and perfected as Pico's method of intellectual ascent 12. The editors add that the mystic's three-stage path was often articulated as purgatio, illuminatio, unitio or perfectio 13, and that the Dionysius of the Celestial Hierarchy was thought to be the follower of Paul mentioned in Acts 14.

Stop 2 — The Heptaplus's nine orders and the postponed Hebrew angelology

In the Heptaplus Pico reads the firmament set between the waters as the three hierarchies, the highest of which "has leisure only for contemplation" and is symbolised by the waters above the heavens, while the middle rank, assigned to the work of the heavens, is symbolised by the firmament 15. He distributes the three angelic activities across the ranks, with the lowest order purifying, the middle illuminating and the highest perfecting with a fiery and life-giving dew, and cites Dionysius for it 16. Copenhaver's Dionysian account of the top hierarchy and Pico's distribution in the Heptaplus assign the same three activities, the first among thrones, cherubim and seraphim and the second across the three hierarchies (ed.).

Pico says "we know of nine orders of angels", that each order has chosen its own leader by lot, and that the leader is to be imagined as a great sphere, with the army that follows as its inhabitants and ornament 17. Howlett says that only nine realms of the celestial world are active, reflecting both the nine-plus-one variant of the Tree of Life and the nine levels of angels 18. Black notes an inconsistency: the division of the universe into angelic, celestial and terrestrial would lead one to equate the empyrean with the angelic world, but Pico treats it as the foremost celestial sphere, the ruler of the other nine, "the monad which fills the denary" 19. Pico also reports that Isaac the philosopher takes the tenth sphere to be Ezekiel's sapphire in the likeness of a throne 20. The disputes below turn in part on the number of orders, nine or ten (ed.).

Black finds that Dionysius plucks words from various biblical places and concentrates on the ranks of angels and God, where Pico follows one chapter of Genesis in sequence and treats the whole cosmic taxonomy below God 21. Pico writes that much about the angelic nature was handed down by the ancient Hebrews and by Dionysius, and that it was his plan to expound Moses according to both schools 22. In Black's account he had first intended two expositions of the angelic world, but for lack of space and time concentrates on Dionysius alone and plans another book on the Jewish doctrine, in which he will sift what accords with Catholic faith from what does not 23. The Hebrew orders are promised in the Heptaplus and never delivered there (ed.).

Pico calls on Dionysius and Thomas for the doctrine that angelic intelligence is superior to human because it comprehends with fewer forms, and Black reports that intelligible forms are bound to an angel's intellect indivisibly and perpetually, but only loosely to the human 24. The translators' note, attached to the proem of the Second Exposition, refers the doctrine to Caelestis hierarchia VII and to Aquinas, Summa theologiae I, question 89, article 1 25. Black, who reports that Garin also refers it to De caelesti hierarchia 7, answers that "the specific subject of the perception of simple forms by the angelic intellect is not present in that chapter" and that the argument is in On the Divine Names 26. The two annotations disagree about which Dionysian treatise lies behind the doctrine, and nothing in the claims settles it (ed.).

Stop 3 — The Hebrew hierarchies of the Conclusiones

Pico says that in the Kabbalistic books he found the fall of the demons and the hierarchy of the angels, the same things read every day in Paul, Dionysius, Jerome and Augustine 27. The thesis, as Copenhaver translates it, reads: "There are nine hierarchies of angels, whose names are Cherubim, Seraphim, Hasmalim, Haiot, Aralim, Tarsisim, Ophanim, Thephsarim, and Isim" 28. Wirszubski quotes the thesis as secundum secretam doctrinam sapientum Hebraeorum Cabalistarum, and it is cited as 1K2 by Copenhaver and as 28>2 by Howlett, whom this essay takes to mean the same thesis (ed.). Copenhaver observes that the Areopagite's nine ranks became canonical for Christians while Jewish tradition often preferred ten groups, and that in a Kabbalist text known to Pico the names were those Pico used, in a different order; Pico's own list puts Cherubim before Seraphim 28.

Wirszubski takes the unusual number nine instead of ten, and the unusual form Aralim for 'Er'elim, to point to Abraham Axelrad's Corona Nominis Boni, in Vatican Hebrew codex 190, as the likeliest direct source 29. The source accounts for Pico's notion of nine hierarchies "just as there are nine celestial hierarchies in Dionysius Areopagita", the different order of the names notwithstanding, though Wirszubski records the coincidence only in parentheses and does not say whether Pico chose or noticed it 30. Wirszubski argues that in the Latin text of Axelrad's source "the tenth grade cannot possibly be taken to denote an angelic hierarchy", so that the inference from that text is nine, whereas ten is the usual number in Jewish sources 31.

Against this stands Howlett, who states that "Pico changes Pseudo-Dionysius' hierarchy of angels from nine to ten, the number of the sefirot", and whose note cites only Conclusion 28>2 for the change 32. Wirszubski, who prints the Latin of the thesis and of Axelrad's source, infers nine hierarchies and treats the tenth grade of that source as no hierarchy 31; Copenhaver's translation of the thesis likewise lists nine 28. The tenth grade belongs to Axelrad's list and not to Pico's thesis, which names nine (ed.). Howlett's ten may rest on another passage, such as the Isaac passage of the Heptaplus, but her note names only the one thesis (ed.).

The one name-for-name pairing in the claims consulted is Copenhaver's: the Hasmalim are the Thrones, and the immutable angels are Seraphim and Cherubim 33. For the sefirot, the editors of the Oration report a suggestion from Farmer that Pico consciously overlaid the sefirot on the Dionysian hierarchy, with the seraphim corresponding to Hesed (love), the thrones to Din (judgment) and the cherubim to Binah (intelligence) 34. The editors introduce it with "It has been suggested" and then describe how the arrangement applies at this point of the text 34. Howlett sets seraphim, cherubim and thrones together in the first triad of Keter, Hokmah and Binah 35. The two placements of seraphim and thrones differ, and each is one scholar's (ed.). Wirszubski adds a narrower pairing: the Orphic Curetes that Pico equates with the Dionysian powers require a Kabbalistic work related to the Fear of Isaac, the fifth sefirah, Might or Judgment 36. The editors' report of Farmer sets Din beside the thrones, and Wirszubski sets the fifth sefirah beside the powers; nothing in the claims says whether these are the same sefirah or reconciles the two placements (ed.).

The editors of the Oration also say that a connection between the names of the angels and the doctrine of the sefirot might be alluded to in the words about the most hidden mysteries, and cite Yates for it 37. The claims that speak of the two systems together say that they share a number, a hint, or a suggested overlay; none says Pico drew up a table (ed.). Pico names the Dionysian orders as Dionysian and the Hebrew list as Hebrew (ed.).

On Michael the editors say Pico identifies Michael, the high priest, with the sefirah Keter, which he takes as the symbol of the priesthood 38. Copenhaver reads the first Kabbalistic thesis as introducing Michael, stationed below the loftier spirits but a high priest nonetheless, whose place in theosophical space is S4, "just beneath the supreme triad where annihilation in the Godhead waits" 39. The two cannot both be right, since Keter belongs to the supreme triad and Copenhaver puts Michael beneath it (ed.). Neither claim carries an argument that decides between them (ed.).

Stop 4 — Enoch, Metatron and Pallas

Pico says that the most secret Hebrew theology "at one time transforms holy Enoch into an angel of divinity", whom they call by a Hebrew name, and at other times reshapes other men into other spirits 40. Copenhaver notes that the speech has said nothing yet about Kabbalah and supplies none of the context, so Christian readers could not have grasped the hint 41. The editors point to the Commento, where Pico says almost in the Oration's terms that when the Cabalist wise men say Enoch was transformed into Metatron they mean that any man may be transformed into an angel 42. They quote Scholem on the two traditions combined in Metatron, one of a heavenly angel created with the world and one that makes him the Enoch who ascended and was changed from a man into an angel 43. For Enoch they judge it more likely that Pico draws on a complex apocryphal tradition than on a single book, possibly including the Third Book of Enoch, where Garin and Tognon refer to the Ethiopic Book of Enoch 40.8 4445.

The first printed edition leaves a blank where the Hebrew word should stand, and the two Basel editions supply a Hebrew back-translation of the Latin words angelum divinitatis 46. Wirszubski says the printed editions inadvertently obscure Enoch's transformation into Metatron, and quotes the only known manuscript, Palatinus 885 in the Biblioteca Nazionale Centrale, Florence 47. He allows that the printed Hebrew may well translate angelus divinitatis, but prefers the manuscript "not because it is manuscript" and because Pico's Commento warrants its authenticity 48. Following him, Bausi restores in his edition the characters of Palatino 885, possibly one of two drafts Pico prepared in the autumn of 1486, and reads them as Metatron 49. The blank in the Oration is thus filled by an argument from a manuscript and from the Commento, and not by a printed witness to the Hebrew word (ed.).

The other blank lies in a thesis and not in the speech: in the tenth thesis of the set following Pico's own opinion, which Copenhaver cites as T2K10, a space for a Hebrew word is left in the printed edition 50. Wirszubski notes that Francesco Giorgio assumed as a matter of course that the missing word was Hokmah, Wisdom 51. Ebgi, on Copenhaver's report, prefers Hokmah, the second sefirah, following a commentary by Zorzi; Copenhaver judges that Wirszubski's Metatron "seems better" because the Agent Intellect, explicitly identified with Metatron by another thesis, better matches the other entities named 50. Wirszubski's own objection to Hokmah is that it would have Pico identify the symbolic images of the pagan ancient theology with Jesus Christ, against the warning in the Commento about the Hermetic and the Christian Son of God, so that either Pico is inconsistent or Giorgio is out of tune with Pico 52. Howlett records, for her part, that in Pico's concordance Hokmah is also the Aristotelian active intellect and Christ 53. The dispute is over the word in the thesis and is separate from the Enoch passage in the Oration, though Metatron is at stake in both (ed.).

Pico named Pallas in the cherub passage of Stop 1, and the editors say that in this thesis and in another, cited as 2.5.14, the Orphean Pallas is associated with the cabalists' Metatron, and that Pallas signifies the intellectual nature which leads to wisdom 54. They describe the order of Pallas as cherubic contemplation that fulfils the link between love, the seraphim, and justice or law, the thrones 55. Copenhaver, with a hedge, infers that because the thesis identifies "what Orpheus names Pallas" as Metatron within the Seraphic fire surrounding God, it must be Pallas Athena who ties the Cherub's knot, presiding as the womanly Wisdom at S2 56. The claim leaves open how this fits: Metatron stands in the Seraphic fire and yet Pallas governs the Cherubic order 56. Copenhaver adds that Pico also learned mysteries from Proclus, who replaced Pallas with Hecate in the Chaldean theology 57. Copenhaver reads the thesis as identifying Pallas with Metatron, where the editors say only that the two are associated, and whether the figure belongs to the cherubic or the seraphic rank is left unsettled by the sources (ed.).

Copenhaver says that Abulafia urged followers to turn into separate angels by attaining prophecy and identified Metatron, the angel prince, with the Agent Intellect, so that union with the Intellect or Metatron eradicates the self 58.

Stop 5 — Sefirot and the three worlds: Black against Wirszubski and Kristeller

In Black's description the sefirot are ten hierarchical emanations of divine power which connect the inmost aspect of God to the created universe, each named after an attribute of God 59. Wirszubski says Recanati's Kabbalah is mainly concerned with the ten sefirot as divine emanations, and Abulafia's with the names of God 60. Many Kabbalists, he adds, took the three triads of sefirot to represent the three stages of being, Intellect, Soul and Nature 61. Howlett places the angelic mind of Ficino's Christian Platonic universe at the first triad, Keter, Hokmah and Binah, which she also takes to be the Trinity for Pico 62. Black's description makes the sefirot emanations of divine power, and Howlett's makes the first triad the angelic mind; this essay keeps the two vocabularies apart (ed.).

Pico says Moses represents the three worlds, the intellectual or angelic, the celestial and the corruptible, by the head, the breast and the lower part of the great man, the angelic world being the fountain of knowledge because that nature was made for understanding 63. In Black's account the highest world is characterised by light, fire, perpetual life and stability, the lowest is its opposite, and the third world is moved by the second and the second by the first 64.

Kristeller reports that Wirszubski proves what had merely been surmised, that the doctrine of the three worlds in the Heptaplus "is of Kabbalistic origin", though it presupposed both the Platonist distinction between the intelligible and the visible and the Aristotelian distinction between celestial and sublunar 65. Wirszubski's own conclusion is more cautious: at the present state of research it is "impossible to pinpoint Pico's direct source", but the analogical representation of the three worlds descends from a Kabbalistic source, be it Bahya ben Asher, Isaac of Acre or Baruch Togarmi 66. His claim is confined to the analogical representation, which is narrower than Kristeller's summary of the whole doctrine (ed.).

Black thinks no single source possible or desirable, and aims to redress the balance towards a broadly Judeo-Aristotelian perspective on the threefold cosmos, "not overly beholden either to Platonism or kabbalah", and calls the model an instance of Pico's syncretism, a model that could be linked to Aristotelian, Platonic or Jewish ideas according to context 67. He finds little evidence that Platonists structured the cosmos as Pico does, and notes that Proclus's threefold model in the Elements of Theology is not consonant with Pico's 68. He allows that Aristotle's ontology at the start of Metaphysics XII matches Pico's three worlds, but thinks a direct link implausible without later developments in the Aristotelian tradition 69. On the sefirot, Black argues that the connection with the spheres is one of shared structure, that the complex of names and attributes which let them serve as an exegetical tool is entirely absent from the Heptaplus, leaving only the number ten, and that "The threefold structure itself... does not require a kabbalistic reading to make sense of it", the application of sefirotic metaphysics to it probably achieving the opposite 70. Links between Pico's cosmic structure and the sefirot, he thinks, can be inferred from the Conclusiones but are not explicit in the Heptaplus and do not contribute to its comprehension 71.

Wirszubski argues from the second thesis of Pico's own set, which equates the three worlds with the triple merkavah, and from the fiftieth thesis, that the three worlds are a triadic grouping of sefirot; Black finds this entirely possible for the Conclusiones but "cannot detect any use of this symbolism in the Heptaplus" 72. What Pico assumes in a set of theses need not be what he uses in an exposition written afterwards, and the disagreement may be only that (ed.). Wirszubski himself says Kabbalah is avoided in the main body of the Heptaplus, understandably, because the Conclusiones were still banned, though the closing interpretation of the first word of Genesis is eminently Kabbalistic 73. Black likewise notes that the final section abandons allegory for a technique of letter combination derived from kabbalistic exegetical works 74. On the closing section the two agree, and the dispute concerns the structure of the worlds (ed.).

Stop 6 — What Pico could read, and what remains open

Around 1486 Mithridates translated for Pico a substantial corpus of Hebrew texts into Latin, and the surviving part amounts to over 3,500 pages in five Vatican manuscripts 75. Pico's association with Mithridates apparently ended before March 1489, for reasons that remain unclear 76. Black concludes that "we cannot assume that, even in 1489, he would have been able to read complete kabbalistic treatises unaided", and that Pico approached kabbalah first through the translated corpus and oral contact 77.

Wirszubski warns that the inventory of Pico's library, the authors he mentions and even the translations made for him may lead us astray, and "the only evidence that can be absolutely trusted is the internal evidence of the theses themselves" 78. Wirszubski says that the ultimate source of the thesis that no angel with six wings is ever transformed is undoubtedly the Zohar, and that there is no need to look beyond Recanati, who quotes it, for the immediate source 79. Wirszubski argues that behind Pico's picture of Kabbalah as the divinely revealed explanation of the Law there is an intervening Kabbalistic source, that Abulafia is the obvious and only choice, and that De Secretis Legis is the only book Pico is known to have read before the Apology that couples these ideas with divine names and combination of letters 80. That book states a world-year corresponding to 1280 CE 81. Howlett says Pico is the first to use the Bahir directly, where Ficino references it through Paul of Burgos 82. Vatican Hebrew codex 189, with the twelve books De Anima ascribed to Eleazar of Worms, was written, Wirszubski argues from Mithridates's notes, when Pico was already in Rome and after the consequences of the publication of the Conclusiones, so that Pico could not have read it before completing his theses 83. For the ladder of the Oration Copenhaver suggests, with a hedge, that a Book of Imaginary Circles by Ibn al-Sid al-Batalyawsi, known to Jews in Pico's Italy, may have been more useful to Pico 84.

Several things stay open (ed.). The dates of Axelrad's Corona Nominis Boni and of the other Kabbalistic works named here, apart from the world-year of 1280 in Abulafia's De Secretis Legis, are not established by the sources consulted (ed.). Whether Pico noticed, chose or merely inherited the coincidence of nine Hebrew and nine Dionysian hierarchies is not said by Wirszubski 30. Howlett says Pico changes the Dionysian hierarchy from nine to ten 32, while Wirszubski infers nine hierarchies from the text he prints 31, and the two are not reconciled here (ed.). Michael's sefirah is Keter for the editors and S4, beneath the supreme triad, for Copenhaver 3839. The blank in the Oration is filled with Metatron in Bausi's restoration from Palatino 885 49, and the blank in the tenth thesis with Metatron by Wirszubski, whose choice Copenhaver thinks seems better, and with Hokmah by Ebgi 50. The three worlds are Kabbalistic in Wirszubski's and Kristeller's account and multiple in Black's 6567. The doctrine of the fewest forms comes from Caelestis hierarchia VII according to the translators and from On the Divine Names according to Black 2526. Two of these turn on a Hebrew word missing from the printed text, and the Hebrew angelology promised in the Heptaplus is not delivered there (ed.).
